The first order

A promising message

At nine on Monday morning, Lina received the message she had been waiting for.

"We would like you to make our new price cards. Could you have them ready by Friday?"

The message came from a small flower shop near the station. Lina had recently started designing printed materials for local businesses. Until then, most of her work had been birthday invitations for relatives, who paid her with dinner and asked for changes over dessert.

This would be her first proper order.

She typed, "Of course!" Then she stopped with her finger above the send button.

How many cards? What size? Did Friday mean Friday morning or the end of the day?

She deleted the answer and asked whether she could visit the shop that afternoon.

Paper and flowers

The owner, Ruth, was carrying a bucket when Lina arrived. She pointed toward a counter covered in ribbon, scissors and small pieces of paper.

"Those are the old cards. We need something clearer."

Lina picked one up. The price was written in pale green, with a flower above it. From the other side of the counter, she could barely read the number.

"Will you use them inside or in the window?"

"Both. And outside when the weather is good."

Lina wrote that down.

They counted twelve kinds of plant that needed permanent cards. Ruth also wanted blank cards for changing offers. She expected the price to include printing. Lina had been thinking only about the design.

For a moment, she wished she had already said yes. It would have felt easier than explaining what she did not yet know.

Instead, she closed her notebook halfway.

"I can price the design today. I need to ask the printer about suitable paper and delivery before I can give you a total."

Ruth nodded.

"Please do. I don't want to discover on Thursday that printing costs extra."

They agreed that ready meant delivered to the shop before ten on Friday morning. Ruth would send the final names and prices that evening. Lina would prepare one sample before laying out the whole set.

The sample

The printer recommended a firm card for indoor use and explained that ordinary paper would not last outside in rain. Ruth already owned clear holders, so Lina measured those before leaving.

That evening, she sent a written quote. It listed the design, one round of changes, the number of printed cards and the delivery time. Extra copies would cost more. Ruth accepted it the next morning.

Lina opened her drawing program and spent an hour making a border of tiny leaves.

She liked the leaves. She tried them in three colours.

Then she printed the sample at its actual size and placed it across the kitchen table.

The leaves looked lovely. The price looked small.

She made the price larger and removed half the border. When she sent the sample to Ruth, she included a photograph of it in a holder, beside a ruler.

The reply arrived during lunch.

"Much easier to read. Can the shop name be a little smaller? And that plant costs eight euros, not six."

Lina checked the list. It said six.

She began a reply explaining this, then decided a phone call would be quicker.

"I can change it," she told Ruth. "Could you check all twelve prices now, so I use one final list?"

There was a pause, followed by the sound of a drawer closing.

"Yes. Two others changed last week. I'll send a new list and mark it final."

A late addition

By Wednesday afternoon, Ruth had approved the complete set. Lina was about to send the file to the printer when another message appeared.

"Could we add a large poster for the window? Same style. It should be quick."

Lina looked at the time. She wanted Ruth to recommend her to other shops. She also knew that making a poster meant checking another size, arranging more text and getting another printing price.

She called the printer first.

Then she answered Ruth.

"The cards can still be ready on Friday. I can also design the poster, but I need the wording and approval separately. I can send you a price for Monday delivery."

Ruth chose to wait until the following week. She had not realised the printer needed a separate file.

Lina sent the cards to print and ate the lunch she had left beside her keyboard. It was nearly four. The cheese had gone warm.

Friday morning

At half past nine on Friday, Lina carried a flat box into the flower shop. Ruth cleared a space on the counter. Together, they checked the quantities and compared the printed prices with the approved list.

One holder was narrower than the others. Its card would not slide in.

Lina felt her face grow hot. Then Ruth turned the holder around and pulled out an old piece of cardboard stuck inside.

"Found the problem."

The new card fitted.

Ruth put three cards in the window and stepped outside to look. Lina followed her.

"Yes," Ruth said. "I can read those without my glasses. Send me the invoice."

On her way home, Lina bought a sandwich. She sat on a bench and opened her notebook to a clean page.

At the top, she wrote, "Next order."

Underneath, she added three questions about size, quantity and deadline. Then, remembering the warm cheese, she put a lunch break into her calendar.
