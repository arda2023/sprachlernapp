Why a smaller shop can win

Walk into a large American superstore and you may find groceries, clothes, electronics and household equipment under one roof. An Aldi shop offers a different experience. The building is smaller, the choice more limited and many products sit in the boxes used to deliver them. What looks like a lack of ambition is part of a deliberate business model.

The interesting question is not whether a small shop can imitate a giant one. It is whether customers will accept fewer choices and services in exchange for a convenient trip and an attractive bill. Aldi's growth in the United States suggests that many will. Understanding that growth requires looking at the work behind the shelves, not just counting new buildings.

Two branches of a family business

The Aldi story began with the Albrecht family's grocery business in Germany. Brothers Karl and Theo developed the discount approach, and the business eventually separated into Aldi Süd and Aldi Nord. These became distinct groups rather than two regional names for one unified operation. The Aldi stores discussed here belong to the southern group's American business.

A frequently repeated story explains the split through a disagreement about selling cigarettes. Such an anecdote is memorable, but it should not stand in for a complete account of management decisions. A business division can involve personalities, organisation and competing preferences. The commercial lesson does not depend on pretending that one disputed product explains everything that followed.

International expansion also came through existing businesses. Hofer became part of Aldi Süd in Austria in 1968. Acquiring an established chain can provide shops, staff and relationships that a newcomer would otherwise need to build. It does not remove every cost or risk, but it changes the starting point for entering a market.

Across the Atlantic

Aldi opened its first American store in Iowa in 1976. The move brought a compact grocery format into a market where many customers were familiar with much broader assortments. Success would require more than copying signs and shelving. The company needed to understand local demand and build a dependable system for getting products into stores.

A shop is only the visible end of that system. Warehouses, transport routes, purchasing and inventory planning decide whether the shelf contains what the customer came to buy. Expanding into a new region therefore means organising a network, not simply finding an empty building and opening the doors.

That helps explain why geographic concentration can matter. Several stores supplied through an efficient distribution network may be easier to operate than isolated locations scattered across a huge country. There are limits to that advantage, including capacity and travel times. A growing chain must keep adapting its support network as its footprint expands.

The American business developed over decades. It was not an overnight conquest in which one German idea immediately overturned established shopping habits. Customers had to learn what the stores offered, and the retailer had to earn repeat visits. A low price may attract someone once. Reliable products and a useful range are what make another trip worthwhile.

Where the savings come from

Limiting the assortment reduces complexity. Instead of stocking many brands and package sizes for every item, a retailer can concentrate purchases on fewer lines. That can simplify ordering, stocking and forecasting. The tradeoff is real: customers looking for a particular brand or specialist ingredient may need another shop.

Aldi places substantial emphasis on its own brands. Its supplier information describes roughly ninety percent of the core range as private label and expresses a preference for working directly with manufacturers or growers. It also requires products to arrive in cases suitable for display. These are practical features of the operating model, not merely an advertising slogan about being inexpensive.

Displaying goods in delivery cases reduces the need to handle each unit when replenishing shelves. Repeating that saving across products and stores can reduce work. It does not mean employees are unnecessary. Someone still orders, receives, checks and sells the goods, cleans the premises and helps customers when problems arise.

Private labels also change who controls the product and its presentation. They do not mean the retailer manufactures everything itself, or that every supply chain contains a middleman who can simply disappear. Contracts, transport, processing and quality control still cost money. Buying arrangements can reduce some costs without making the underlying work vanish.

Customers take on part of the routine too. Aldi's American checkout process generally leaves customers to pack their groceries after payment. Bringing bags and handling the packing can make sense to shoppers who value the resulting experience. For others, extra service elsewhere may be worth paying for. Different formats serve different preferences.

Price pressure and changing habits

When household budgets tighten, grocery spending becomes an obvious place to look for savings. People who rarely compared supermarkets may begin checking their receipts. A discount format can benefit from that attention, especially if customers discover that its products suit them. However, an economic crisis does not automatically explain every new store or guarantee lasting loyalty.

Inflation also needs careful interpretation. If inflation slows, prices can still be rising, just less quickly. Households may continue to feel pressure from earlier increases even after the rate falls. That helps explain why interest in value can persist. It does not establish that one retailer is always a fixed percentage cheaper than another.

A fair comparison needs a defined basket, place and date. Package sizes, quality, promotions and product availability can change the result. A striking claim that Aldi is thirty percent cheaper than Walmart everywhere would promise more than such evidence can support. The useful question for a shopper is what their own regular purchases cost.

Competition without a simple winner

Store improvements can make the discount offer more appealing while preserving its basic operating choices. Brighter spaces and a clearer layout need not eliminate delivery boxes or a limited assortment. The challenge is to improve the experience without adding costs that undermine the reason customers came in the first place.

Lidl's American arrival in 2017 added another competitor using a discount approach. Its launch announcement described twenty locations planned for that summer and a goal of up to one hundred stores by the following summer. That was an expansion plan, not a statement that more than one hundred shops opened on the first day.

Competition with Walmart is also more complicated than a story of one chain opening while the other only closes. In January 2024, Walmart announced plans to build or convert more than 150 stores over five years. Individual closures can coexist with investment elsewhere. Different store formats can grow at the same time without one replacing the other completely.

In January 2026, Aldi announced plans for more than 180 American openings that year, aiming for nearly 2,800 stores by its end and 3,200 by the end of 2028. These figures describe dated company plans. They should not be turned into completed totals for an earlier month or guaranteed future revenue.

One final distinction concerns Trader Joe's. According to its own ownership explanation, it belongs to families that also own part of Aldi Nord, but operates independently. It has no business or ownership relationship with Aldi Süd, including Aldi in the United States. Familiar family connections do not make every store part of the same operation.

Aldi does not have to become a complete substitute for every superstore to succeed. Its proposition is narrower: make a useful grocery trip work with fewer complications and controlled costs. Whether that proposition wins a household's weekly shopping depends on the products, the location and the bill. The smaller building is only where that larger system becomes visible.
