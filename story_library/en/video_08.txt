Buying Hollywood

In October 2026, the companies behind Batman, Harry Potter, CNN and CBS came under the same corporate roof. Paramount completed its acquisition of Warner Bros. Discovery, and the combined company took the name Skydance. A business that had recently been much smaller than its target had managed to buy it. The explanation lies in the difference between what a company is worth on the stock market and how much money it can persuade other people to provide.

The deal carried an announced enterprise value of about 110 billion dollars. That number included debt as well as the value paid for shares. It did not mean that Paramount had kept 110 billion dollars in a bank account, waiting for the right studio to appear. The purchase depended on investors, lenders and the expectation that the enlarged business would earn enough money to support its obligations.

A different route into film

David Ellison entered Hollywood with advantages few aspiring actors could imagine. His father, Larry Ellison, had helped establish Oracle and accumulated an enormous fortune. In 2006, David appeared in Flyboys, a film about American pilots in the First World War. The film struggled commercially. His work as a producer proved more successful than that early appearance suggested.

He founded Skydance in the same year. The company later worked with established studios on major productions, including films in the Mission Impossible series and Top Gun: Maverick. Producing meant helping assemble the money, people and agreements that made a film possible. Success in that role offered a route towards owning more of the business itself.

In 2025, the Ellison family and investment firm RedBird helped bring Paramount under new control. The acquisition gave the group a famous film studio, the CBS television network, children's programming and a streaming service. Ellison now led a company whose history reached far beyond his own production business. Soon, an even larger opportunity emerged.

A valuable library with bills

Warner Bros. had spent a century building a film and television catalogue. Its characters and stories could earn money through cinemas, television, games, merchandise and streaming. A successful film might attract audiences long after its first release. Owning a library also made it possible to offer subscribers something familiar while new productions were being developed.

Yet a valuable catalogue did not remove financial pressure. Warner had passed through several corporate combinations, including Time Warner and AOL. AT&T acquired Time Warner in 2018, then moved away from its attempt to combine telecommunications with a large entertainment business. In 2022, WarnerMedia joined Discovery. The resulting company carried substantial debt into a changing television market.

For years, cable television had provided dependable income. Households paid for bundles containing many channels, including some they rarely watched. Channels received distribution revenue and sold advertising. This arrangement helped fund expensive programmes and supported large companies with regular cash receipts.

Streaming weakened that model as viewers cancelled traditional subscriptions. Building a streaming service required spending on programmes, technology and marketing, while customers could leave after watching the series they wanted. The established business was shrinking before its replacement had necessarily become equally dependable. Debt payments continued regardless of how audiences chose to watch.

In June 2025, Warner Bros. Discovery announced plans to divide into two listed companies. One would contain studios and streaming, including HBO. The other would contain much of the television network business. The proposed split made the different parts easier to evaluate. It also raised an obvious question for potential buyers: should they acquire the growing attractions, the whole group, or nothing at all?

Two different offers

Netflix pursued the studio and streaming assets. Paramount wanted the whole company. This difference mattered as much as the headline price. An offer for selected businesses could leave shareholders with an interest in the remaining networks and their associated financial obligations. An offer for every share promised a different outcome, with cash replacing the shareholder's ownership of the entire group.

Paramount's early approaches failed to persuade Warner's board. It then took an offer directly to shareholders, trying to win support without the board's recommendation. Such an approach is often described as hostile. The word refers to the relationship with the target's leadership, rather than to anything unlawful about making an offer.

Price alone was not enough. Shareholders also needed confidence that a buyer could obtain the money and complete the transaction. A higher promise could be less attractive if it was unlikely to reach closing. Financing commitments, contractual protections and the cost of abandoning an earlier agreement therefore became part of the contest.

In February 2026, Paramount offered 31 dollars per share. Warner's board judged the proposal superior under its agreement with Netflix. Netflix declined to increase its offer, saying that matching the new price would no longer make financial sense. Losing an auction can be a deliberate decision to protect a business from paying more than it expects to recover.

Where the money came from

The financing plan announced with the agreement included 47 billion dollars in new equity. Investors would receive newly issued shares in exchange for providing capital. These were funds brought into the purchase by investors, rather than profits that Paramount had accumulated from selling cinema tickets or subscriptions.

Banks and other lenders also committed substantial financing. These commitments included arrangements connected with existing obligations as well as additional borrowing. Adding every financing headline together does not produce a simple cash purchase price. Some facilities replace or support other facilities, and the enterprise value already includes debt belonging to the acquired business.

New equity and borrowing create different claims. Shareholders own a portion of the business and accept the risk that their investment may lose value. Lenders expect repayment under agreed terms. Issuing more shares can reduce the proportion owned by existing shareholders. Borrowing avoids that particular effect but creates payments that must be met even in disappointing years.

This is how a smaller company can acquire a larger one. Its current stock market value is not a spending limit. Outside investors can supply capital, while lenders assess the cash the combined business might generate. The buyer gains access to the target's earnings, but it also inherits exposure to its problems.

Control is a separate question. Different classes of shares can carry different voting rights. Someone who contributes a large amount of money may receive a substantial economic interest without gaining equivalent influence over management. Understanding who owns a business therefore requires looking at both the financial stake and the rights attached to it.

After the signatures

Signing the agreement did not immediately transfer the company. Approvals and closing conditions still had to be satisfied. The acquisition was completed on October 6, 2026. That distinction matters whenever a proposed merger appears in the news. A signed contract describes an intended transaction. Closing establishes that it has actually happened.

Management presented the combination as an opportunity to improve production and reduce duplicated costs. Such savings may come from shared systems, offices or distribution. They can also involve difficult decisions about employment and investment. Announced savings are targets, and their eventual size depends on decisions that have yet to produce results.

For viewers, a corporate merger does not automatically combine subscriptions or put every film into one application. Rights agreements, existing services and commercial plans still shape what can be watched. For employees and creators, the consequences may be more immediate as a smaller number of decision makers influence budgets and commissions.

The acquisition brought an extraordinary collection of entertainment businesses together. It also left the new management responsible for making an expensive financial plan work. Films must still find audiences, subscribers must still decide to stay, and lenders must still be paid. Buying a famous name can be completed on a particular day. Earning enough from it remains an ongoing task.
