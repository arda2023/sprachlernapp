A paper map

The blue doorway

The photograph showed a woman sitting on a low wall, holding an enormous ice cream. Behind her was a blue doorway with the number seventeen above it. Someone had written "Our first summer" on the back.

"Your grandmother hated that picture," Ben said.

His daughter Ada turned it over again.

"Why?"

"She said I should have told her the ice cream was on her nose."

Ada looked more closely. There was a white spot, small enough to miss until somebody mentioned it.

They were sitting outside the tourist office in a town Ben had last visited almost forty years earlier. Ada had suggested the stop when they planned their holiday. Her grandmother couldn't travel with them, but she had asked for a photograph of the same doorway, if it still existed.

The trouble was that Ben remembered the ice cream shop, the sea and an argument about a sunhat. He did not remember the street.

"Near the church," he said.

Ada looked across the square. There were two church towers visible from where they sat.

"That narrows it down."

A circle in pencil

Inside the tourist office, a young man studied the photograph while an older colleague unfolded a paper map.

"The blue door may not be blue anymore," she said. "And seventeen could be on almost any street. Is there anything else in the picture?"

Ada pointed to a curved shadow across the wall.

"A sign?"

"Possibly. Let me see."

The woman held the picture farther away, then closer. Ben recognized the movement. He did the same thing with restaurant menus.

"This might be the old bakery lane. It had a low wall like that. Start here."

She circled a narrow street in pencil.

"Go past the fountain, take the second street on the right, then look for the steps. There's a small printing shop at the top. The owner knows that part of town well."

Ada opened the map on her phone.

"This lane isn't labelled."

"It's barely wide enough for two people. The paper one is easier for the little streets. Keep it."

Ben accepted the map and immediately folded the circled street inside it. Ada took it back and folded it the other way.

"You can carry the photograph," she said.

Number seventeen

They found the printing shop above a steep flight of steps. In the window were wedding invitations, notebooks and a sign asking customers to close the door because the cat was not allowed outside.

The cat was asleep on a stack of cardboard boxes.

The owner looked at the photograph without touching it. His fingers were stained with dark ink.

"The old ice cream shop," he said.

Ben smiled.

"I knew it was near here."

"It was. But the door is on the other side of the building. People always photographed the side entrance. The front had an ugly metal shutter."

He drew a short route on their map, avoiding the freshly printed sheets on the counter.

"Back down the steps, turn left and go through the arch. You'll see a green door."

"Green?" Ada asked.

"Unless they've painted it again."

They thanked him and stepped outside. Ben was already looking around for the arch.

"Down first," Ada reminded him.

The doorway was green. Above it, a pale rectangle showed where a number had once been fixed. The low wall remained, although a large plant pot covered one end.

Ben stood still in the lane.

"That's it."

Ada held up the photograph and compared the stonework. One stone beside the door had a corner missing. The same gap was visible in the picture.

"You're right."

A different photograph

There was a notice on the door: private entrance, please keep clear. Ada moved back toward the opposite wall and checked that she wasn't blocking the lane.

"Stand where she was. I'll get the angle."

Ben sat on the low wall, then stood up again.

"Someone lives here."

"We can just photograph the door."

Before she could take the picture, it opened. A woman carrying a shopping basket looked from Ben to Ada.

"Can I help you?"

Ben explained the photograph. He held it out, and the woman's expression changed.

"My aunt worked in that shop. May I look?"

She studied the image for a moment.

"That's the old sign's shadow. We found the bracket when we repaired the wall."

Ada asked whether they could take a picture from the lane. The woman agreed and moved her basket out of view.

"You should sit down," she told Ben. "Otherwise your mother will say you found the wrong place."

"She might say that anyway."

Ada took three photographs. In the first, Ben blinked. In the second, a delivery trolley appeared beside his knee. In the third, he was laughing at something the woman had said.

They chose the third.

Something to send home

The ice cream shop had moved to a street near the harbour. The woman explained the way, and Ada marked it on the paper map. Ben bought two small cones. He held his carefully away from his shirt.

They sat by the water and called Ada's grandmother. Ada showed her the old photograph first, then the new one.

"The door was much nicer before," she said.

"Dad found it."

"With several assistants," Ben added.

His mother moved closer to her camera.

"Did you find the little printing shop too?"

Ada looked at Ben.

"You knew about that?"

"Of course. That's where we bought the postcards. I thought you remembered."

After the call, Ada wrote the street name on the back of a fresh postcard. She added the date and the name of the ice cream shop. Ben folded the map along its original creases, taking his time.

"Are we keeping that?" she asked.

"Certainly. It has the route on it."

This time, the pencil circle stayed on the outside.
