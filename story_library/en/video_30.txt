Beyond the burning streets

An empty place in the classroom

The trouble began with things a school was supposed to provide: teachers, usable rooms and enough space to learn. In September 2026, pupils at a school in Créteil, southeast of Paris, joined a protest over conditions inside their building. According to the documentary, staff positions were empty, water leaked into rooms and some classes lacked enough tables and chairs.

Teachers went on strike. Pupils blocked the entrance. Their first demand was not for a revolution. They wanted their school to work. A missing teacher meant a lesson that could not happen as planned. A damaged classroom was something they faced each morning, not a distant argument about public spending.

Local officials visited, and talks began with education authorities. The documentary reports that appointments were announced for vacant positions. That was a practical gain for the school. Yet by then, videos of the protest had travelled far beyond its gates. Other pupils recognised the complaints and began sharing their own.

The protest spreads

Across the Paris suburbs, pupils organised further blockades. Calls circulated through social media, where a short video could reach people who had never heard of the original school. The images carried a straightforward message: these problems were not confined to one building.

Student organisations called for wider action. Complaints included missing teachers, crowded classes and buildings in poor condition. Some also objected to the pressure of school schedules and uncertainty about entering higher education. The demands varied, but many pupils described feeling that decisions about their future were being made without them.

Blocking a school could attract attention quickly. It also interrupted lessons and created arguments about who should be allowed through the entrance. The protests did not follow one pattern everywhere. Some gatherings remained calm. Elsewhere, confrontations and damage changed the situation around the gates.

The documentary describes fireworks being directed at police and officers using force against young people. Images of these encounters spread as quickly as the original appeals for support. For pupils who said they were demonstrating peacefully, the police response became another reason to protest.

What the images could show

A video could record a troubling moment without explaining everything that happened before it. That mattered when people tried to judge who had started a confrontation or why an officer had acted. A person appearing in the same frame as a violent act was not necessarily responsible for it.

The case of Bally Bagayoko, the mayor of Saint Denis, showed how much a clip could leave out. Footage of him near a burning rubbish bin circulated during the protests. Some viewers treated it as evidence that he was encouraging or causing the fire.

A published comparison of longer footage and eyewitness accounts gave a different explanation. The scene had taken place on 28 September, outside a school. The bin was already burning when the mayor approached. He moved it away from the gates. Images that appeared to support an accusation became much less convincing when the earlier moments were included.

That correction did not settle every argument about the mayor or his party. It settled a narrower question about a particular scene. Across the protests, the same care was needed with claims about students, police officers and people who had arrived to cause damage.

More people join

By October, the demonstrations were drawing support from outside the schools. Parents and teachers joined young people in the streets. Other participants were concerned about public services, wages and the rising cost of everyday life. They did not all have the same demands, even when they walked together.

Members of the yellow vest movement also called for action. That movement had emerged in 2018 amid anger over fuel costs and taxes. In 2026, some of its supporters saw a connection between their own difficulties and the students' complaints. Both groups questioned whether people with limited money were being heard.

For pupils, that support could make a local problem feel less isolated. Adults were publicly saying that damaged classrooms and missing lessons mattered. The parents federation FCPE called on families to join the mobilisation. Its support reflected a position in the dispute, rather than an independent judgment on every confrontation.

As 17 October approached, calls for gatherings appeared across France. Shopkeepers in some areas prepared for possible damage. Police prepared for large crowds. Organisers hoped that the different groups would give greater weight to their demands. No one could know in advance whether every gathering would remain peaceful.

A crowded square

On 17 October, large demonstrations took place in Paris and other cities. Students marched alongside adults concerned about education and living costs. Reports described separate causes coming together in the same streets, with people carrying signs and explaining why they had joined.

In Paris, the Place de la République became a central meeting point. The monument at its centre gave the gathering a familiar setting for public protest. For many participants, being there was a way to make themselves visible and to support people whose problems resembled their own.

But the peaceful demonstrations were followed in some places by violent confrontations. The documentary shows broken windows, fires and clashes with police. Shops belonging to small businesses were damaged as well as outlets of large chains. Those losses did not disappear because the wider protest had understandable demands.

Nor did the damage establish that everyone who had marched supported it. A pupil asking for a teacher, a parent accompanying a child and a person breaking a window could be present in the same city for very different reasons. Treating them as one group made the pictures simpler and the explanation worse.

Whose account would prevail?

Political arguments intensified. Government representatives accused violent groups of exploiting the movement. Supporters of the students accused the authorities of responding to legitimate grievances with excessive force. Each new confrontation supplied more images for an argument that was already bitter.

The documentary also describes reactions from politicians outside France. Some tried to explain the unrest through migration or ideological conflict. Such claims required evidence about the people and events involved. Images of a burning street could not, by themselves, establish a national explanation.

The pupils' original concerns risked becoming harder to hear. News coverage understandably followed injuries, fires and arrests. But a school with too few teachers could remain in difficulty after the cameras moved elsewhere. The spectacle of a confrontation did not reveal whether a missed lesson would be replaced the following week.

After the demonstrations

The government held meetings with student representatives and opened a consultation about school life. During October, the education ministry announced measures concerning teacher replacements and student participation. These were responses to the dispute, but an announcement could not by itself demonstrate an improvement in a particular classroom.

Further proposals were announced on 28 October, including changes intended for later school years. Those commitments belonged to the future. Their effect would depend on what was funded, organised and delivered. Students and parents still had reasons to look beyond the announcement and ask what would happen at their own school.

By the end of the month, the story was still unfolding. Calls for protests elsewhere in Europe did not prove that the same events would follow in another country. The experience in France already showed something more concrete: a complaint about everyday schooling had brought many different people into the streets, while violence and political argument threatened to obscure what had brought them there.

For the pupils, the test would come when they returned to class. Was there someone to teach the lesson? Was the room usable? Did anyone listen when the same problem happened again? Those questions had started the protest, and the dramatic images had not answered them.
