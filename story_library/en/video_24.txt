The letters of Monsieur X

A journey broken in the dark

Late on 17 October 1977, the Italia Express was passing Hecklingen in southwestern Germany. The train was travelling from Copenhagen towards Rome. Some passengers were sitting awake, others were in sleeping compartments. Then the train derailed. A journey that should have continued through the night ended with damaged carriages, injured people and emergency workers arriving in darkness.

This was no ordinary railway accident. Someone had interfered with the track. The derailment injured passengers and a railway employee, with serious injuries among those affected. Near the scene, investigators found a message associated with a name they already knew: Monsieur X. For almost two years, that signature had appeared in threats against the Deutsche Bundesbahn.

The name concealed a person, but it also connected a series of events. There had been letters demanding money, damage to railway infrastructure and arrangements for payments that never went through. The crash at Hecklingen made the danger unmistakable. People who had never heard of the correspondence were now suffering its consequences.

A demand presented as a loan

The documentary traces the beginning to a letter received by the railway administration in Karlsruhe in October 1975. Its author demanded 100,000 German marks. He described debts and plans for a new business, and even offered to repay the money with interest. The presentation resembled a request for financial help until the threats began.

The writer referred to a family, wartime disability and a desperate situation. Those were claims made by an anonymous author, not facts the railway could simply accept. He warned that refusal would bring attacks on trains and damage to the network. Whatever language he used about repayment, the demand depended on the threat of violence.

He also wanted a public response. A newspaper advertisement would show that his letter had been received and that the railway was engaging with him. The arrangement gave the anonymous writer a way to obtain a reply without providing a normal return address. It also forced the authorities to consider how a public message might affect what he did next.

An incident on the railway followed shortly after the first letter. In another message, Monsieur X claimed responsibility. Investigators were dealing with more than words on paper. They needed to protect travellers while finding someone whose identity remained unknown and whose next action could take place along a large network.

Appointments that led nowhere

The source describes a series of attempted handovers. Instructions arrived, a courier waited, and the expected contact failed to happen. On another occasion, a telephone conversation ended before the arrangement could proceed. The promised exchange of money did not produce an arrest or bring the threats to a close.

Monsieur X claimed to know that police were involved. Such statements could intimidate the recipients, but the claim itself did not establish everything he knew. Investigators had to work with an uncomfortable uncertainty. A failed appointment might reflect suspicion, confusion or a changed plan. They could not read the anonymous writer's private reasoning from the outcome alone.

Then the messages stopped for a time. Silence offered no reliable assurance that the danger had passed. In 1976, a freight train derailed near Rastatt, and further letters appeared. The author explained his absence by saying he had been ill. Again, that explanation belonged to his own account. It was not an independently confirmed medical history.

The demand rose to 250,000 marks, and the writer sought a response over the radio. The public communication served a familiar purpose: he wanted evidence that the people receiving his demands were listening. The railway and police had to manage that communication while the possibility of another attack remained.

By 1977, further damage to railway infrastructure had widened the investigation. Some problems were discovered before a serious accident occurred. Others damaged railway equipment. Patrols and inspections could reduce danger, but a railway covers long distances and operates through the night. Watching the whole system was a different task from guarding a single building.

Objects, words and a voice

The attacks left physical material as well as written messages. The documentary describes investigators tracing components through a seller who remembered a customer. That recollection contributed to a description of a possible suspect. It was a lead to follow, rather than a photograph of someone caught committing an attack.

The letters created another kind of evidence. Their vocabulary, grammar and repeated expressions could be compared. Investigators could ask whether separate messages appeared to come from the same author and, later, whether they resembled writing known to belong to a suspect. Those are related questions, but they are not identical.

Written language analysis also differs from examining handwriting or the marks made by a typewriter. One concerns how someone uses language. Another may concern the physical production of a document. Treating all these methods as a single test makes the result sound simpler and more certain than the work actually is.

Recorded telephone calls added the speaker's voice. Accent, pronunciation and patterns of speech can provide useful information. The documentary describes specialists identifying regional influences and comparing them with the life of the eventual suspect. A resemblance can support an argument, but it does not turn an accent into a unique identity card.

This distinction is especially important in a case that later became a story about successful detection. Once a suspect's biography is known, earlier clues can seem to point neatly towards it. The court still has to consider the strength and limitations of each comparison. A convincing narrative about the investigation is not itself another piece of evidence.

A letter in Strasbourg

After the Hecklingen derailment, the search continued through further messages and public appeals. The documentary's decisive change of direction comes from a hotel in Strasbourg. A letter connected with a planned handover was left there. When a man later collected it, the hotelier followed and noted the registration of his vehicle.

According to the account, the importance of this information was not recognised immediately. The connection was made later, after publicity about the case on the television programme Aktenzeichen XY. An investigator recalled the Strasbourg incident, and the vehicle information became a route towards a named person.

The episode shows a practical difficulty of a large inquiry. Information can exist without being connected to the question it helps answer. A public appeal may matter because it prompts someone to reconsider an earlier observation. That does not mean the television broadcast proved the case. It helped bring a lead back into view.

On 17 February 1978, investigators arrested Hermann Kraft in Freiburg. The anonymous name now had a suspect attached to it. Kraft, however, denied being the railway attacker. The investigation had reached a person who could be questioned and whose movements could be examined. It had not removed the need to establish the accusation in court.

The man who denied it

The documentary describes Kraft as a trained printer who later worked with ornamental fish and aquarium supplies. He made and delivered products, living a life that did not outwardly resemble the threatening figure in the letters. That contrast gave the story attention, but an ordinary occupation could neither prove nor disprove involvement in serious crime.

Investigators also examined his visits to casinos and possible connections between his journeys and the attacks. Financial circumstances and travel could help test an account. They could not, by themselves, establish that gambling caused the offences or that being near a railway meant carrying out sabotage. Each alleged connection needed its own support.

Kraft said he had acted for an unknown private detective. That explanation became part of the dispute over how to interpret his actions. The prosecution argued that he was Monsieur X. The defence challenged the case against him. The central question was no longer whether the railway had been attacked, but whether the accused was responsible.

The proceedings relied on circumstantial evidence. Such evidence can support a conviction, but its value comes from what it establishes and how the pieces fit together, not simply from the number of items collected. Weak points do not become strong merely because they appear beside one another. Nor does the absence of a confession automatically prevent proof.

In 1979, Kraft was convicted and sentenced to life imprisonment. The court accepted that he was the man behind the name Monsieur X. He continued to deny it. The documentary draws heavily on a book later written by the prosecutor, which explains the investigation from the prosecution's perspective. That perspective should remain visible when the case is retold.

After the verdict

Years later, in 2007, journalist Patrik Müller interviewed the man who had been convicted. The documentary includes Müller's recollections of that meeting. He described someone marked by imprisonment, worried about recognition and still committed to his account of innocence. The newspaper report used a substitute name, which should not be mistaken for a different person in the case.

The interview did not reopen the evidence by itself. A continued denial is part of the person's account, not proof that the conviction was wrong. Equally, the existence of a conviction does not require an outside storyteller to present every expert statement or investigative theory as beyond question. The legal outcome and the limits of the available sources can both be stated clearly.

There is also a danger in allowing the mystery of the author to overshadow the people on the train. They had not chosen to participate in a dispute about money. The damage to the railway became injuries, fear and interrupted lives. Their place in the story does not depend on whether later accounts focus on a clever clue or a disputed comparison.

Monsieur X tried to control events through anonymous messages. The investigation answered with documents, witness observations, recordings and comparisons that eventually reached a courtroom. The result was a conviction, followed by a denial that lasted long afterwards. The story remains a reminder of both the harm an anonymous threat can cause and the care required when turning clues into a claim about a person.
