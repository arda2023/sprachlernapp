The first evening

Before the doors opened

At five forty, Leo tied his apron for the third time. The knot felt too loose, then too tight. He was trying again when Hana came out of the kitchen carrying a stack of menus.

"The apron isn't the difficult part", she said.

"I'm practising on something I can control."

She gave him four menus and pointed toward the tables along the wall.

It was his first evening serving at the restaurant. During his training that afternoon, he had learned where the glasses were, how to enter an order and which door led to the storeroom rather than the toilets. Now the chairs were straight, the candles were lit and somebody was about to expect him to know what he was doing.

Hana placed a small plan of the dining room beside the till.

"Tables one to four are yours tonight. I'll be nearby. If you don't know an answer, ask."

"What if I forget a table number?"

"Look at the plan. That's why it's there."

He traced the row with one finger.

"One, two, three, four."

"Good. Now put water in the jug. Guests tend to notice an empty one."

The first order

The first guests were a woman and a boy carrying a book almost as large as his chest. Leo showed them to table two.

"Would you like something to drink?"

"Sparkling water for me", the woman said. "And apple juice for him."

The boy looked up.

"Without ice, please."

Leo wrote it down. When he returned with the drinks, he put the juice beside the book and kept the glass away from its open pages.

The boy moved the book onto the spare chair.

"It's about bridges."

"Are you building one?"

"Not a real one. Yet."

The woman ordered fish with potatoes. The boy chose pasta with tomato sauce.

"Can the cheese come separately?" she asked.

"Yes. I'll put that on the order."

Leo read everything back, including the cheese. At the till, he checked the table number before entering it.

Hana passed behind him.

"You can breathe while you type."

He let out the breath he had been holding.

Two similar glasses

By half past seven, all four tables were occupied. Leo had stopped adjusting his apron. He had also discovered that a pen could disappear into a pocket he had checked twice.

At table three, two friends ordered lemonade. One wanted ice and one did not. Leo brought both glasses on a tray and remembered the difference until he reached the table.

Then he forgot which person had asked for which.

For a moment, he considered putting the glasses down and hoping.

"Sorry, who ordered the lemonade without ice?"

The person by the wall raised a hand.

"Me."

"Thank you."

Nothing else happened. Nobody sighed. Nobody demanded another waiter. He set down the glasses and went back for the cutlery he had promised table four.

When he told Hana, she looked up from the reservation book.

"You asked a question and got the answer. That's usually quicker than getting it wrong."

At the kitchen counter

A bell rang. Two plates waited at the kitchen counter, and Leo reached for the one closest to him.

"Which table?" the cook asked.

Leo looked at the ticket.

"Two. Fish and pasta."

"Then these aren't yours. These are for five. Yours are coming next."

He pulled his hands back.

Hana collected the plates for table five. Leo stayed beside the counter until his order appeared, then checked both dishes against the ticket. The small bowl of cheese stood beside the pasta.

He carried the plates one at a time because he was not yet comfortable carrying two. On the second trip, he brought the cheese as well.

"Anything else I can get you?"

"A little more water, please", the woman said.

When he returned with it, the boy was drawing a bridge on the paper table mat.

"That's our street", he told Leo.

"It doesn't have a river."

"It will in the drawing."

The extra coffee

Later, table one asked for the bill. Leo printed it and checked the list before taking it over. There were three coffees on it. He remembered serving two.

He looked at the order history, then called Hana.

"I think I entered one twice."

She checked with him and removed the extra coffee using her supervisor access. He printed the corrected bill and checked it again.

"Should I have noticed earlier?"

"Earlier would have been useful. Before asking them to pay is useful too."

At table one, the guests paid and asked whether the restaurant opened on Sundays. Leo knew this answer without checking. He told them the opening hours and wished them a good evening.

After the rush

At ten fifteen, Leo sat at an empty table with a glass of water. His feet hurt, and the pen had left a blue mark on his thumb.

Hana sat opposite him with the dining room plan.

"Anything you want to go over before tomorrow?"

"Carrying two plates."

"We can practise with cold ones before opening. Anything else?"

"I nearly took food to the wrong table."

"Yes. Keep reading the ticket before you pick anything up."

He nodded and wrote that in the little notebook he had brought from home.

As they cleared the last table mats, he found the boy's drawing. A wide bridge crossed a river between the restaurant and a tall building. Underneath, the boy had written a name for it.

Leo held it up for Hana.

"We appear to have a new address."

She examined the drawing.

"At least he left us a way across."

Leo put it beside the till, where they could offer it back if the family returned. Then he untied his apron. This time, he managed it on the first attempt.
