Behind the prison doors

A letter asking for help

The doctor had worked at Augsburg Gablingen prison for only a few months. What she described after leaving was serious enough to require an answer from people outside its walls. In October 2023, she reported suspected abuse and asked for help. Her warning reached the National Agency for the Prevention of Torture and the Bavarian justice ministry.

For the doctor, sending the warning did not bring relief. In the documentary, she recalls a long period of waiting while hearing that conditions were getting worse. She worried about the prisoners and about staff who were struggling with what was happening around them. Having raised the alarm, she felt unable to stop anything herself.

Gablingen was a relatively new prison, opened in 2015 near Augsburg. It housed adult men, including people awaiting trial as well as people serving sentences. Its modern buildings and security systems offered little help in answering the question that would eventually dominate public discussion. What happened when a prisoner was taken somewhere that almost nobody outside could see?

Ordinary accommodation for one prisoner must be distinguished from the special secure cells at the centre of the allegations. A person can have a room alone without being subjected to the exceptional restrictions described in this case. The disputed cells were places of much more severe separation, used when prison authorities considered someone dangerous to themselves or others.

Such a measure requires a concrete justification. It cannot become an informal punishment for being difficult, complaining or asking for medical attention. Yet that is close to what several former prisoners alleged had happened to them. Their accounts, the doctor's warning and later official findings would gradually bring the prison under intense scrutiny.

The visit in August

On 9 August 2024, a delegation from the National Agency arrived without an appointment. The agency visits places where people are deprived of their liberty and reports on their treatment. It had already inspected Gablingen in November 2022 and made recommendations. This return visit was partly intended to find out what had changed.

The delegation entered the prison immediately but then waited twenty minutes in the reception area before meeting the deputy director. After a shortened introductory discussion, its members asked to see the special secure cells. The distinction matters. The report describes a delay inside reception, rather than inspectors being refused entry at the outside gate.

The cells were in the basement and had no windows. The inspectors found the air particularly poor. The ministry later pointed to a ventilation system that exchanged the air regularly, but the agency maintained that the conditions it had experienced could place an additional burden on someone already in crisis. It considered the rooms unsuitable for confinement lasting several days.

There were mattresses in the cells when the delegation visited. The paperwork did not provide the same reassuring picture. References to mattresses were rare, and one recorded that a prisoner received one only after two days. The agency inferred that confinement without a mattress appeared to have been the usual practice. That conclusion came from comparing the records with what was visible during the visit.

The report also recorded a suspicion that mattresses and paper underwear had been supplied before the inspection reached the cells. It asked for that possible deception to be investigated. The suspicion should not be presented as something the visitors watched happen. They saw the rooms as they were shown to them and later questioned whether that picture was representative.

Other findings were direct and specific. The records described at least eight instances in which prisoners had been held without any clothing, with no reason recorded for requiring them to remain naked. Camera observation continued. Paper underwear seen by the delegation in other cases was transparent even where privacy was most necessary.

The toilet area appeared on the camera image without pixelation. The agency criticised the resulting intrusion and called for decisions about observation to be justified individually. It also recommended appropriate bedding. These were findings about conditions and safeguards. They were not criminal verdicts establishing the guilt of particular employees.

What prisoners described

In the documentary, a former prisoner introduced by the nickname Totti recalls being transferred to Gablingen while suffering from severe back pain. He says that requests for treatment did not produce the help he expected. His distress increased, and he eventually found himself in a special secure cell rather than receiving the care he had hoped for.

His description of the room is stark. He says there was no clothing, no mattress and almost nothing around him. Food arrived without a plate, and he had to put it on the floor. He remembers trying to make lying on the hard surface less painful with what little material he had available. These are his allegations, not observations made by the filmmakers inside the cell at the time.

He also describes uncertainty about how long he would remain there. In an ordinary sentence or a specified disciplinary measure, a person may at least know the expected duration. His account conveys the distress of having no clear answer. That uncertainty does not mean the authorities had an unrestricted legal right to hold him there indefinitely.

Another prisoner, Gunter, describes a confrontation after trying to report an insulting note. According to his account, officers struck him and subsequently took him to a secure cell. He says the injuries were visible afterwards and that many members of staff must have seen his condition.

The officers gave a different account. They said he had attacked them and that they restrained him in response. The documentary reports that a local court convicted Gunter of resisting officers and assault. It also cites reporting that questioned how his injuries were addressed. That earlier case cannot simply be rewritten from a television interview, nor does it settle every later allegation about the prison.

These competing accounts show why the evidence matters beyond the force of a speaker's recollection. Medical records, contemporaneous notes, witnesses and the sequence of decisions may help establish what happened. A prisoner's criminal history does not make every complaint false. Equally, the seriousness of an accusation does not remove the need to test it.

The prisoners' accounts also raise a practical difficulty. A person inside a cell cannot walk away from the people responsible for their custody. Complaints may pass through the same institution whose conduct is being questioned. Access to an independent listener is therefore part of the story, alongside the alleged violence itself.

The security group

Much of the investigation focused on a specialised group of prison officers and the former deputy director. Such teams can be called when staff need help dealing with dangerous situations. Their work may involve force, but the purpose and limits of that force remain important. A difficult assignment cannot justify punishment outside the law.

The former doctor describes the deputy as a powerful figure in the prison. She recalls changes in responsibilities and a working atmosphere in which some staff felt intimidated. Other accounts alleged that the deputy and members of the security group protected one another. These descriptions form part of the accusations, rather than a finding that every employee shared the same knowledge or intentions.

According to the documentary, some prisoners alleged assaults in places where other staff could not easily observe what was happening. Others described humiliation during searches or restraint. The relevant question is not whether a prison needs security procedures. It is whether particular actions were necessary and lawful, or whether that authority was being used to harm people.

An operation at another prison brought further allegations into view. On 23 October 2024, officers from Gablingen assisted at the youth prison in Neuburg Herrenwörth during a search for drugs. Prisoners there later described being assaulted during the operation. The subsequent prosecution account also included allegations relating to that visit.

The documentary describes the head of the Neuburg prison notifying the ministry. The next day, searches took place at Gablingen. The allegations were no longer circulating only through individual complaints and warnings. Investigators were gathering material, and journalists began speaking with people who said they had been affected.

For the doctor, the public attention arrived after months of frustration. For former prisoners, it offered the possibility that accounts they felt had been dismissed would finally be examined. Neither reaction answers the criminal charges, but both help explain why the case carried such emotional weight for those who spoke publicly.

The warning and the response

The handling of the doctor's original letter became an issue of its own. The ministry said it had referred the allegations to prosecutors. That referral was an action, but it did not end the questions about what officials did with other complaints or how they followed developments inside the prison.

There had been a preliminary examination before the searches in October 2024. The decision at that earlier stage not to open formal proceedings must be distinguished from an acquittal after a trial. It did not establish that every complaint was false. Later information could lead investigators to a different assessment.

It is also necessary to separate an institution receiving a warning from a particular minister personally reading it. The letter's arrival at the ministry supports questions about its internal handling. By itself, it does not prove exactly what every senior official knew on a particular day.

The National Agency's report raised another concern about oversight. It criticised a ministry letter asking it to avoid unannounced visits in future. The agency rejected that request, explaining the importance of being able to inspect without advance notice. An inspection arranged entirely around an institution's convenience may miss conditions that an unexpected visit could reveal.

Its own August visit nevertheless showed the limits of a brief inspection. Rooms can be examined, documents requested and staff interviewed, but the visitors cannot observe every previous shift. The agency said its documentation was incomplete and requested more. The ministry indicated that further files would be provided after an extension of the deadline.

Messages and denials

In May 2026, reporting by BR and Kontraste described messages attributed to members of the security group and prison leadership. According to the journalists, some messages appeared to celebrate mistreatment, discuss keeping actions acceptable in appearance or suggest that colleagues protected one another.

The reporting made the allegations more disturbing because it raised the possibility of deliberate conduct rather than isolated mistakes under pressure. But readers of the story are encountering the journalists' account of those records. The entire conversations, their attribution and their relationship to particular events still require examination as evidence.

One former prisoner reacted with anger and disbelief. He had felt that nobody believed him when he first complained. Hearing reports of messages that appeared to resemble his experience made that dismissal harder to bear. His response is understandable as a personal account, without turning the reported messages into an automatic confession to every charge.

The deputy director's lawyers disputed the accusations. They argued that their client believed she had acted lawfully and criticised what they regarded as public prejudgment. They also questioned the handling of the investigation and portrayed her as someone being made to carry responsibility for wider institutional problems.

Those arguments belong in the account alongside the allegations. A failure to answer a journalist's questions is not an admission of guilt. Nor should an insulting public label be treated as a medical description or a legal conclusion. The defendants remain entitled to challenge the evidence and have their individual conduct assessed.

The case reaches court

In January 2026, prosecutors filed charges against the former prison director, her former deputy and a former member of the security group. A second indictment in April concerned ten additional former members of that group. This meant thirteen accused people across two indictments, not thirteen convictions or thirteen separate indictments.

The alleged responsibilities differed. Prosecutors accused the former deputy of using secure cells without adequate justification and of involvement in abuse. Allegations against the former director concerned unlawful confinement and failures in supervision. The January announcement specifically distinguished her position from participation in the physical assaults described there.

The two indictments also overlapped in their accounts of events and participation. Adding all their numbers together would give a misleading impression of separate incidents or separate victims. The court joined the proceedings in August 2026 because the underlying events substantially overlapped.

In September, the court admitted both indictments and opened the main proceedings. That updated an important point in the documentary, which had ended while the decision was still pending. Admission means the case will proceed to trial. It does not mean that the judges have already found the defendants guilty.

As of 30 October 2026, the trial was scheduled to begin on 7 January 2027. The published schedule listed many hearing days and anticipated further dates. It was a timetable for hearing evidence, not a promise of a particular verdict or a guaranteed finishing date.

For those who say they were harmed, the proceedings offer a chance to have their experiences examined in public. For the accused, they provide the setting in which the prosecution's account must be tested. The questions about supervision and the handling of warnings also remain, even though they cannot all be reduced to a single person's criminal responsibility.

At the end of the documentary, the former doctor says she intends to attend the trial. One former prisoner hopes for recognition of what he says happened, perhaps even an apology. Neither describes the approaching hearings as an abstract legal exercise. They want answers about particular rooms, particular decisions and particular moments when someone asked for help and felt that nobody listened.
