Full planes, thin profits

A full aircraft looks like a successful business. Nearly every seat has been sold, passengers have paid for bags, and the crew is preparing to leave. Yet the airline may earn very little from the journey. Once fuel, wages, maintenance and other bills have been paid, only a small part of the money collected may remain. Sometimes the flight loses money despite carrying plenty of people.

That problem became especially visible in 2026. In its June outlook, the International Air Transport Association expected the world's airlines to make a combined net profit of 23 billion dollars. Its earlier forecast had been 41 billion. The revised figure represented a net margin of roughly two percent and about four dollars and fifty cents per passenger. These were industry forecasts, not the final results of every airline or the profit from each individual ticket.

The average concealed large differences. Some companies had stronger finances, better routes or protection against rising fuel prices. Others had entered the year struggling. On May 2, Spirit Airlines announced that it was winding down operations and cancelled its flights. The company cited higher oil prices and other business pressures, and said no additional funding was available. Fuel had worsened an existing financial problem rather than explaining the entire failure on its own.

A seat with an expiry date

An airline sells something it cannot put back on a shelf. Once an aircraft departs, an empty seat on that flight can never be sold. The opportunity has disappeared. This gives airlines a reason to accept lower fares when they expect seats would otherwise remain empty.

But pricing is more complicated than making every ticket cheap. Airlines try to estimate demand, separate different customer needs and decide when to offer lower fares. A passenger booking a holiday months ahead may pay less than someone who needs to travel tomorrow. A busy route with limited competition can support different prices from a route where several airlines compete for the same travellers.

Many costs remain even when bookings disappoint. The aircraft must be financed or leased. Crews must be available, maintenance must be carried out, and airports charge for their services. Carrying another passenger adds some costs, but leaving a seat empty does not remove the airline's main obligations. This is why filling an aircraft can help without guaranteeing a profit.

Revenue figures also need care. Airlines earn money from more than passenger tickets. Cargo, baggage fees and other services contribute to the total. Dividing all industry revenue by the number of passengers does not reveal the average ticket price. It mixes different sources of income into one number, which can make a simple comparison misleading.

Competition changed the business

For much of the twentieth century, governments played a large role in deciding how airlines operated. National carriers connected countries with the wider world. Some routes were important for trade, tourism or public policy even when their direct financial return was weak. Ownership and public support varied between countries, but aviation was often treated as more than an ordinary consumer business.

The United States began a major change with the Airline Deregulation Act of 1978. Economic restrictions on routes and fares were removed over time, giving airlines more freedom to compete. Safety regulation remained. Deregulation did not mean that anyone could simply operate an aircraft wherever they wanted without certification or access to airport facilities.

Competition encouraged cheaper fares and different business models. Airlines focused on costs could attract travellers who had previously found flying too expensive. Established companies had to respond. Passengers gained more opportunities to travel, while airlines faced continuing pressure to offer prices that would cover their costs without sending customers to a rival.

The fuel problem

Fuel is a large expense whose price can change quickly. An airline needs a specific refined product, not simply crude oil. Refinery capacity, transport routes and local availability affect what it pays. A disruption can therefore make jet fuel rise differently from the oil price shown in the evening news.

Conflict and transport disruptions in the Middle East put heavy pressure on fuel markets in 2026. Airlines could adjust schedules and fares, but they could not control the international energy market. Nor could they immediately replace every aircraft with a more efficient model. Changes to a fleet take time and require capital.

A simple example shows the exposure. Suppose fuel accounts for a quarter of a company's costs. If its fuel price rises by seventy percent while consumption and all other costs stay unchanged, total costs rise by seventeen and a half percent. An airline keeping only a few percent of revenue as profit has little room to absorb that increase. This is an illustration, not a calculation of every airline's actual results.

Higher fares can pass some costs to customers, but there are limits. Travellers may postpone holidays, choose another destination or decide not to fly. Tickets already sold cannot usually be repriced just because the fuel bill has increased. A company must therefore manage the gap between what it expected to spend and what it now has to pay.

Buying some certainty

One way to reduce uncertainty is hedging. Through financial contracts, an airline can protect part of its expected fuel purchases against price changes. Ryanair reported that, at the end of March 2026, it had hedged about eighty percent of its estimated fuel exposure for the following financial year. That provided protection for a defined period and volume.

Hedging does not remove every risk. Contracts expire, actual fuel needs can differ from forecasts, and protection arranged before a price fall may leave an airline paying more than competitors buying at the new market price. The aim is often to make future spending more predictable. It is not a permanent promise of the cheapest fuel.

Delta took another approach when its subsidiary Monroe Energy bought a refinery near Philadelphia in 2012. Owning refining capacity gave the airline more influence over part of its supply chain. It still had to obtain crude oil and operate the facility. A refinery also has maintenance costs and business risks of its own. Ownership cannot make an airline independent of energy markets.

When governments intervene

During major crises, governments sometimes provide loans, guarantees, grants or support for wages. They may want to preserve jobs and transport connections, or prevent a sudden collapse from spreading through the economy. State ownership can also make an airline politically important. None of these arrangements guarantees that public money will always be available.

The pandemic showed both the scale of intervention and the need to distinguish different forms of aid. Lufthansa received government support during the collapse in travel. It repaid the German government loans and deposits it had received in 2021. Germany sold its remaining shares in 2022. A support package is therefore not automatically a permanent subsidy or continuing state ownership.

Governments also face competing demands on their budgets. Creditors, employees and taxpayers may disagree about whether a rescue is justified. Supporting an airline can preserve useful connections, but it does not by itself repair an unprofitable network or excessive debt. A company still needs a workable business after the emergency money arrives.

The real test

Airline failure has no single cause. Fuel shocks matter, but so do debt, competition, fleet problems, management decisions and access to cash. Bankruptcy proceedings can sometimes allow a company to reorganise and keep flying. A shutdown is a different outcome. Treating every court filing as the end of an airline hides that distinction.

Full planes tell us that people want to travel. They do not tell us whether the fares cover the bills, whether debt is manageable or whether money will be available when payments fall due. Those questions determine how much room an airline has when conditions change. The difficult part of aviation is not just selling the seats. It is keeping enough of the proceeds to operate the next flight.
