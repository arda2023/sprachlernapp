Who shapes our sweet tooth?

Breakfast and the bigger picture

Imagine a breakfast table with crunchy cereal, milk, toast, jam and a glass of juice. Nothing looks unusual. There is no bowl of sweets in the middle of the table. Yet several of the foods may contain substantial amounts of sugar. How much depends on the products and portions. Working out what that means for health takes more than adding every number on the packets together.

Milk contains naturally occurring sugar. So does fruit. Sugar can also be added during manufacturing, cooking or at the table. These categories matter because health recommendations do not treat every food containing sugar as interchangeable. An apple, a glass of juice and a bottle of lemonade have different structures and nutritional qualities, even when some of their sugars are chemically similar.

The World Health Organization recommends keeping free sugars below ten percent of total energy intake. It conditionally recommends a further reduction below five percent for additional benefits. For an illustrative daily intake of two thousand calories, those figures correspond to fifty and twenty five grams. They are not universal limits on all sugar for every person.

Free sugars include sugar added to food and drinks, as well as sugars in honey, syrups and fruit juice. They do not include the sugars naturally present inside intact fruit or in milk. A breakfast calculation that includes all the sugar in plain milk and compares it with the lower free sugar recommendation therefore mixes different measures.

The distinction does not make the problem disappear. It makes it easier to describe accurately. Sweetened drinks and familiar packaged foods can make free sugars easy to consume throughout the day. The question is why reducing that intake has proved so difficult, even when public health organizations have been calling for action for years.

What sweetness does and does not explain

Sweet foods can be pleasurable. They can also be convenient, cheap and available when people are tired or in a hurry. It is tempting to explain every choice through an ancient instinct that modern industry has learned to exploit. Human preferences do have a biological history, but a story about a prehistoric person discovering berries cannot explain the whole modern food system.

Price, advertising, habit and the products available nearby all influence what people buy. A parent choosing food after a long working day is making a decision within those conditions. Describing the result as nothing more than weak willpower leaves most of the situation out of view.

The body uses glucose, but people do not need to obtain it by adding table sugar to meals or drinking sweetened beverages. Many foods supply carbohydrates, and the body has ways of maintaining its glucose supply. That does not mean every sweet food is forbidden. It means the nutritional need for glucose is not an argument for adding sugar without limit.

There are good reasons to pay particular attention to drinks. They can provide substantial energy without the same eating experience as a solid meal. A person may drink something sweet alongside food rather than instead of it. Over time, choices that increase energy intake can contribute to weight gain, although weight is influenced by many factors.

Dental health is another concern. Frequent exposure to free sugars supports processes that cause tooth decay. Brushing matters, but it does not make diet irrelevant. Nor should a discussion of diet imply that dental care is unnecessary. Different preventive measures address different parts of the problem.

Type 2 diabetes is more complicated than the claim that people get it because they eat sugar. Insulin resistance, genes, activity, age and other factors contribute to risk. People should not be blamed for a diagnosis, and type 1 diabetes should not be confused with this discussion. A disease can have preventable risk factors without every case being preventable by an individual's choices.

A systematic review published in the BMJ in 2015 found that higher consumption of sweetened beverages was associated with a higher incidence of type 2 diabetes, including after adjustment for body fatness. It combined prospective observational studies. That supports concern, but it is different from an experiment proving that one extra drink causes a particular person to develop diabetes.

Claims about sugar causing every kind of cancer, dementia or mental illness go beyond what such evidence establishes. Health arguments become weaker when several different diseases are bundled together as if the evidence were identical. The case for reducing excessive free sugar consumption does not require that exaggeration.

Following the money into research

The debate also has a history of commercial influence. In the United States during the 1960s, researchers were examining possible dietary contributors to heart disease. Sugar, fat and cholesterol were all part of the discussion. For the sugar industry, the direction of that discussion had financial consequences.

Decades later, historians of health research examined internal industry documents. Their 2016 analysis described how the Sugar Research Foundation financed a literature review involving Harvard researchers D. Mark Hegsted, Robert McGandy and Frederick Stare. The project was known internally as Project 226. The resulting review appeared in two parts in 1967.

These were not two new clinical experiments. They were an assessment of existing research. According to the historical analysis, the review gave greater weight to fat and cholesterol while discounting evidence concerning sugar. The industry funding was not disclosed in the publication. Disclosure requirements at the time differed from today's, but readers still lacked information that would have helped them assess the relationship.

This history does not prove that every conclusion about saturated fat was wrong. It shows how a sponsor can influence which questions receive attention and how existing evidence is presented. A choice about emphasis can matter without anyone inventing a laboratory result.

Another project, known as Project 259, involved animal research in Britain. A later examination of industry records described work on sugar, intestinal microbes and blood fats, along with findings concerning an enzyme previously associated with bladder cancer. Funding ended, and the historians did not find published results from the project.

Those findings should not be turned into a claim that researchers had proved sugar causes bladder cancer in humans. They had not. The historical question concerns what the industry knew about preliminary results and what happened to research it financed. The interpretation of the funding decision belongs to the document analysis, not to an imagined conversation in an office.

Dental research offered a further example. A 2015 historical study examined industry influence on the American National Caries Program. It found that substantial parts of an industry report entered the programme's first request for research proposals. Attention went toward ways of limiting dental damage while allowing sugar consumption to continue, whereas some research directly relevant to particular foods was left out.

The issue was the research agenda itself. If a programme concentrates on treating the consequences of consumption, it may leave less room for asking how to reduce exposure. That is a different mechanism from falsifying data, but it can still affect the knowledge available to policymakers.

It would nevertheless be inaccurate to say that American dietary advice simply ignored sugar for decades. The federal guidelines published in 1980 already advised people to avoid too much of it. They also discussed fat and other aspects of diet. The historical record is more complicated than a successful industry campaign making every official recommendation say the same thing.

How to read a conflict of interest

Industry funding did not end with those historical cases. Modern research papers often disclose sponsors and other financial relationships. That disclosure is useful, but it does not settle how a study should be judged. The methods, data and interpretation still need examination.

A review published in 2013 compared systematic reviews of sweetened drinks and weight gain. It found a difference between conclusions associated with declared industry conflicts and those without reported conflicts. Reviews in the first group more often judged the evidence insufficient to establish an association. The sample was small, so the much repeated percentages need their denominators to be meaningful.

The finding gives a reason to examine financial relationships carefully. It does not allow a reader to dismiss every industry funded paper or accept every other paper without scrutiny. An undisclosed relationship is a problem, but a disclosed relationship is not proof that a result is false.

Influence can also occur before a paper exists. A sponsor can choose to support one question rather than another. A research programme about physical activity may produce useful work while leaving the role of a company's drinks outside its scope. Asking who chose the question is therefore as important as asking whether the final calculations were done correctly.

The documentary behind this story describes companies supporting health projects and university posts. The institutions it contacted denied interference in research and teaching. Those responses belong in the account. Sponsorship can create a potential conflict without proving that a particular scientist was instructed to produce a particular result.

The same care is needed with public information. A website offering advice on teeth or nutrition may look independent while being funded by an interested organization. Clear disclosure helps readers understand who is speaking. It does not replace an assessment of the advice, but it gives readers information they should not have to discover through an investigation.

The fight for children's attention

Advertising works at a different point in the chain. It does not have to win an argument about blood sugar. It has to make a product familiar, desirable or easy to remember. A cartoon character, a sports star or a favourite online personality can help achieve that.

Children encounter food marketing through more than television. It can appear in entertainment, on social platforms and alongside activities they enjoy. The boundaries between an advertisement and the surrounding content may not always be obvious to the person watching.

In its 2023 guidance, the World Health Organization described evidence that food marketing affects children's preferences, intake and requests to adults. That does not mean every advertisement controls every child. It means that the pattern of exposure is relevant to health and cannot be reduced to whether one parent said yes to one request.

Parents still make decisions. But they do so while companies spend money trying to shape the decisions children ask them to make. A discussion of parental responsibility is incomplete if it treats that commercial influence as part of the natural background rather than a deliberate activity.

Restrictions on marketing are therefore different from banning a family from buying a product. They concern how a product is promoted and to whom. There can be real disagreements over definitions, enforcement and where the line should be drawn. Those disagreements are more useful when the actual measure is described accurately.

Inside the policy debate

In Germany, the interests involved extend beyond sugar producers. Farmers, food manufacturers, drink companies, retailers and advertising businesses may all have something at stake. Their interests are not identical, but several can oppose a measure that would make a familiar product harder to promote or more expensive to sell.

Lobbying is not automatically corruption. Governments need information from people affected by proposed rules, including businesses and workers. The problem is whether access is balanced, relationships are transparent and public health evidence receives an adequate hearing. A meeting with a minister proves access, not necessarily an unlawful agreement.

The documentary interviews Renate Künast, who was responsible for food and agriculture policy in the German government from 2001 to 2005. She recalls strong resistance when she tried to address unhealthy diets and advertising. Her account describes pressure reaching beyond her own ministry. These are her recollections, rather than an independently verified record of every conversation.

The documentary also describes later attempts under Christian Schmidt and Julia Klöckner to negotiate change with industry. In the account concerning Schmidt, he acknowledged that cooperation had been difficult, while qualifying how he understood discussions with party colleagues. That qualification matters. Reporting political pressure should not silently convert every participant's account into agreement about what happened.

Round tables can bring useful knowledge together. They can also leave a government dependent on the willingness of companies to accept changes that affect their sales. The outcome depends on who participates, what commitments are made and what happens if those commitments are missed.

There is a practical difference between a promise and a requirement. A voluntary target may encourage progress, especially when businesses see advantages in changing their products. A binding rule adds consequences for failing to comply. Neither approach should be judged solely by the ambition of its announcement. The relevant question is what changes afterwards.

Reading the packet and measuring progress

Food labels are one way of giving consumers more information. Under European rules, nutrition declarations became mandatory for most prepacked processed foods from December 2016. That was not the beginning of every kind of compulsory food labelling. Other requirements already existed, and the nutrition obligation has exceptions.

The sugar figure on a standard nutrition table describes total sugars. It does not separately tell the shopper how much is free sugar under the WHO definition. The ingredient list can provide additional clues about what has been added, but a shopper should not have to pretend that these two kinds of information answer exactly the same question.

Portion size adds another complication. Imagine two bottles with the same sugar concentration, one twice the size of the other. Drinking the entire larger bottle provides twice as much sugar. Comparing the amount per hundred millilitres helps someone compare recipes, while looking at the amount in the bottle helps them understand what they actually drink. Both calculations are useful, but they answer different questions. A small serving printed on a label may not resemble the serving someone pours at home.

Labels also ask something of the person using them. Comparing products takes time and understanding. Someone shopping with a tired child or a limited budget may not examine every packet. Information is valuable, but its presence does not remove differences in price, availability or marketing.

Even a carefully informed shopper must choose from the food that is available and affordable in that particular shop.

Germany's voluntary reduction strategy provides a more direct example of why measurement matters. A study published in 2023 examined soft drink sales and sugar content from 2015 to 2021. It found that the average sugar concentration, weighted by sales, had fallen by about two percent in Germany. The industry's target was a fifteen percent reduction by 2025, using 2015 as the baseline.

That study was an interim assessment. It could show that the observed pace was slow, but it could not report a final result for a year that had not yet arrived. The distinction prevents an early warning from turning into a falsely dated conclusion.

Using comparable data, the same study found a decline of about twenty nine percent in the United Kingdom over the same period. This was a measure of sugar concentration in soft drinks sold, not a percentage fall in every person's total dietary sugar. It also did not mean that the number of people with diabetes had fallen by that amount.

The phrase weighted by sales matters here. An ordinary average would give a rarely purchased drink the same importance as a popular one. Weighting the figures by sales gives more influence to the products people buy most. The result can change because manufacturers alter recipes or because shoppers buy a different mix of drinks. Looking at the average alone cannot tell us how much each of those changes contributed. Researchers need further information to separate them.

Changing the incentives

The United Kingdom announced its Soft Drinks Industry Levy in 2016 and introduced it in 2018. Its structure gave producers an incentive to reduce sugar concentration, since drinks above specified thresholds faced a levy. A company could respond by changing a recipe rather than only increasing the price.

That mechanism matters because it reaches people who never read a public health campaign. If a commonly purchased drink contains less sugar, consumers may receive less from that product without making a new decision at every purchase. Whether total intake falls also depends on how purchases change and what people choose instead.

A levy is not a complete nutrition policy. Its design affects which products are covered, what substitutes become attractive and how costs are shared. Evaluations need to consider these responses rather than assume that the policy has the same effect on every household.

Suppose a manufacturer reduces the sugar in a drink, but customers begin buying larger bottles. Some of the reduction per glass could be offset by the amount consumed. Alternatively, people might switch to water, adding to the effect of the recipe change. These are illustrations, not claims about what happened in every country. They explain why a useful evaluation follows several measures at once: recipes, sales volumes, household purchases and, where possible, changes in diet. A favourable number deserves attention, followed by a careful look at what it measures.

Chile offers an example of several measures introduced together. Its food policy combined warning labels, marketing restrictions and rules affecting sales in schools. A study of household beverage purchases found a decline in purchases of products classified as high in relevant nutrients compared with the pattern expected from earlier trends.

Purchases of other drinks increased, so the result should not be described as an equally large fall in all drinks or all sugar. Nor could the study isolate the effect of each part of the policy package. The useful lesson is that the food environment can change through several connected measures, and the consequences can be studied rather than merely asserted.

Campaigners also propose better food standards in schools, more opportunities for physical activity and making healthier foods more affordable. These address different problems. Exercise has benefits of its own, but it is not a reason to ignore diet. A healthier school meal does not remove advertising outside school. Combining measures requires attention to how they work together.

A different ending in Germany

The political situation changed after the period described in much of the documentary. On 14 October 2026, the German cabinet backed a proposal intended to introduce a tax on sugary drinks from July 2027. More precisely, it approved wording for an amendment that the governing parliamentary groups could bring into the legislative process.

This was a significant step beyond simply rejecting a levy. It was not proof that a final law had already taken effect. Parliamentary consideration, implementation and later evaluation remained distinct from the cabinet announcement. A story ending with an unqualified claim that the government still refused any such measure would therefore be out of date.

The change does not erase the earlier arguments over voluntary commitments or commercial influence. It changes the next questions. What will the final measure cover? Will companies alter their products? How will prices and purchasing respond? Will the results justify the claims made for it?

For a family at the breakfast table, those questions may feel distant. Yet the products on the table reflect choices made long before anyone opened the fridge. Recipes, prices, advertising and public rules all helped shape what was available. People make decisions within that setting, and changing the setting can make different decisions easier.

The case for prevention does not require treating every sweet food as poison or every person who enjoys one as irresponsible. It requires taking avoidable harm seriously and being honest about what the evidence can show. A policy should be open to criticism, just as an industry's assurances should be open to examination.

The next chapter will be written through those ordinary details: what manufacturers put into drinks, what children are encouraged to ask for, what schools serve and what households can afford. Personal choices remain part of the story. So do the decisions that determine which choices are easiest to make.
