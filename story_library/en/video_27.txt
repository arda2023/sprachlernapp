The return of Flight 705

An ordinary departure

On 7 April 1994, Federal Express Flight 705 left Memphis for San Jose, California. It was a cargo flight, with no cabin full of holidaymakers or business travellers. The people responsible for getting it across the country were Captain David Sanders, First Officer James Tucker and flight engineer Andy Peterson.

There was one other employee aboard. Auburn Calloway, also a flight engineer, was travelling as a passenger. For the working crew, the presence of a colleague did not initially suggest an emergency. They had a familiar job to do, and the aircraft began its journey normally.

The documentary opens with that contrast between routine and danger. Three people were preparing to complete a working day. Within a short time, they would be struggling to stay alive while keeping a large aircraft in the air. Their destination would cease to matter. The only place they needed to reach was the airport they had just left.

The attack

After takeoff, Calloway attacked the crew. All three men suffered serious injuries. The assault transformed a confined working space into a struggle in which there was little room to move and no possibility of simply escaping.

Tucker initially remained at the controls while Sanders and Peterson resisted the attacker. The documentary describes abrupt movements of the aircraft during the struggle. It is enough to understand that the flight was far outside its ordinary routine. Claims of an absolute speed record add certainty that the available account does not support.

The crew faced two emergencies at once. They had to prevent Calloway from taking over, and someone still had to fly. A victory in the struggle would mean little if the aircraft could no longer be brought safely to the ground. Nor could the person flying ignore what was happening only a short distance away.

Their injuries made both tasks harder. This was not a controlled exercise with a known ending. Each man depended on the others to continue doing something essential while hurt. The account is remarkable without requiring invented dialogue or a precise reconstruction of everything they thought.

Turning back

Tucker contacted air traffic control and requested an immediate return to Memphis. On the ground, controllers had to respond to a situation they could hear only in fragments. They could provide directions and prepare an emergency landing, but they could not see the struggle inside the aircraft.

For a period, radio calls went unanswered. Silence did not tell the controllers exactly what had happened. It left them without information at a time when every update mattered. Emergency services were alerted while the aircraft continued toward the airport.

Sanders later made contact and took responsibility for the landing. Getting back within sight of Memphis did not end the danger. The aircraft still had to descend, slow down and reach a runway, while the struggle aboard had not simply disappeared.

The documentary presents the return as a sequence of urgent decisions rather than a neat recovery. A flight that should have continued west instead came back to its starting point. Sanders brought the aircraft down, and responders were finally able to reach those aboard.

All three crew members survived. Calloway was taken into custody. The landing prevented the attempted takeover from ending in a crash, but it did not undo the injuries. Survival was the immediate outcome, not a return to the ordinary day that had begun in Memphis.

What came before

The investigation examined Calloway's circumstances before the flight. He was facing scrutiny at work over reported flying hours. Investigators also examined financial arrangements and documents connected with his family and insurance. These matters became part of the evidence about preparation and intent.

The documentary suggests that he hoped his family would benefit financially from an apparent accident. That account of his thinking should not be confused with a guarantee that an insurer would have paid. Financial pressure and fear of losing work also do not make violence inevitable or explain away responsibility for attacking other people.

The important question was what Calloway had attempted to do aboard the aircraft. The crew's resistance had stopped the takeover. That did not erase the attempt or the danger created for people in the air and on the ground.

The judgment and the crew

A jury convicted Calloway of attempted aircraft piracy and interference with flight crew members. He initially received concurrent life sentences. On appeal in 1997, the additional interference conviction was removed because it was included within the more serious offence. The conviction and life sentence for attempted aircraft piracy remained in place.

That legal distinction does not diminish what the crew endured. It does correct the claim that two separate life sentences for attempted murder and aircraft piracy were the final outcome. Accurate names for the offences matter when telling the story of a real criminal case.

For Sanders, Tucker and Peterson, the consequences extended beyond the courtroom. They had survived severe injuries during an emergency that demanded continued action from all three. The story's successful landing should not conceal that cost.

Years later, their actions were still being recognised. In 2021, the three men were honoured through induction into the Tennessee Aviation Hall of Fame. A FedEx aviation executive who attended described the crew as an example for colleagues facing danger.

Flight 705 never reached San Jose that day. Its achievement was the return to Memphis, made possible by three injured crew members who continued working together. The aircraft stopped on a runway, emergency workers came aboard, and the men who had begun the journey as colleagues left it alive.
