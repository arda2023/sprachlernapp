The wrong suitcase

The blue ribbon

By the time Eva reached the guesthouse, the rain had soaked through her coat. She left a trail of drops across the hall and apologised to the woman behind the desk.

“Everyone brings a little weather inside,” the woman said. She handed Eva a key with a wooden fish attached. “Second floor. Breakfast until ten.”

Eva had come to the coast for three days. No meetings, no family visits, no carefully arranged programme. She wanted to walk beside the sea and eat something she had not cooked herself. On the train, she had even switched off her work phone.

Upstairs, she lifted her suitcase onto the bed. It was dark green, with a blue ribbon tied around the handle. Her sister had suggested the ribbon after Eva had lost her luggage on a flight years before.

The zip caught at one corner. That was strange. Her suitcase always opened easily.

Inside lay a pair of enormous brown shoes.

Eva stood very still. Beneath the shoes were a cream shirt, a folded jacket and a small cardboard box. She closed the lid of the suitcase without touching anything else.

Then she noticed the ribbon. Hers had white dots. This one was plain blue.

A number on a card

She called the railway lost property office. After several minutes of music, a tired man answered. He asked for the train number, the time and a description of the bag. He could record her report, he said, but he could not promise when anyone would contact her.

“My medicine is in mine,” Eva said. “Nothing urgent tonight, but I need it tomorrow.”

He added that to the form.

Downstairs, the woman at the desk listened while Eva explained. Her name was Rosa. She offered Eva a cup of tea, then asked whether the suitcase had a label.

It did. Eva had been so annoyed with herself that she had not thought to check the little pocket beneath the handle. Inside was a business card for a repair shop. Someone had written “Martin” and a mobile number on the back.

The phone rang twice.

“Please tell me you have some very large shoes,” said a man.

Eva laughed so suddenly that she almost dropped her tea.

Martin had her suitcase. He was staying in a village farther along the coast, where his daughter was getting married the following morning. The jacket was for the wedding. The cardboard box held two small cups that had belonged to his parents.

“I thought I would give them to her before the ceremony,” he said. “Then I opened your bag and found a yellow swimming costume.”

They agreed to meet at the station between the two villages. There was a café opposite the main entrance. Eva checked the bus times with Rosa, borrowed an umbrella and went back into the rain.

At the closed café

The bus took longer than expected. At every stop, someone struggled aboard with wet bags. Eva kept one hand firmly around the green handle.

When she arrived, the café was dark. A handwritten notice on the door said the owner had gone home early because of a broken pipe.

Under the station clock stood a tall man with a green suitcase. A blue ribbon with white dots hung from its handle.

They exchanged bags and opened them beneath the station roof. Eva found her medicine in the side pocket. Martin checked the cardboard box. He unwrapped one cup just far enough to see that its thin handle was intact.

“I packed these more carefully than the suit,” he said.

His daughter already had plenty of cups, apparently. He had almost bought her something new instead. Then he remembered how she used to drink warm milk from these when she visited her grandparents.

Eva told him she still owned her grandmother’s ugly orange bowl. She used it every Sunday, although it matched nothing in her kitchen.

“Keep it,” Martin said. “Matching is overrated.”

They checked their buses. His would arrive in twelve minutes. Hers would not come for almost an hour.

Across the road, a bakery was closing. The woman inside saw them looking through the window and unlocked the door. There was no coffee left, she explained, but she could offer tea and the last two cheese rolls. They ate standing at a narrow counter while she washed the trays.

Before Martin left, he took a pen from his pocket and wrote his name on the blue ribbon. Eva borrowed the pen and did the same.

The next morning

Sunlight woke her before breakfast. She put on dry clothes and walked down to the harbour, where fishing boats knocked gently against their ropes. Her phone vibrated.

Martin had sent a photograph of the cups on a kitchen table. Beside them was a woman in a dressing gown, her hair full of clips. She was laughing at something outside the picture.

“She remembered them,” his message said.

Eva sent back a photograph of the sea. Then she put the phone away and followed the smell of fresh bread along the harbour. She still had two whole days, and Rosa had mentioned a path that began just beyond the lighthouse.
