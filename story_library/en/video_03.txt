Beyond the bookstore

A business on the road

In 1994, Jeff Bezos left a well paid job in New York and travelled west with his wife, MacKenzie. He was preparing to sell books over the internet. During the journey toward Seattle, he worked on the business plan from the passenger seat. The company he was imagining would need customers who were willing to order from a screen and wait for a parcel.

Bezos had studied electrical engineering and computer science at Princeton. His early career took him through technology and finance, eventually to the investment firm D. E. Shaw. He had a promising position there when the rapid growth of internet use caught his attention. Leaving meant giving up a career that was already going well for an idea whose customers barely existed yet.

Books offered a practical starting point. There were far more titles than an ordinary shop could keep on its shelves. A website could present a much larger selection. Books were also standardised products: a customer searching for a particular title could identify it without first holding it or trying it on.

The name Amazon suggested the scale Bezos hoped to reach. The early operation was much smaller. Desks made from doors became part of the company's story about saving money. Staff had to build the website, handle orders and work out how to get books to buyers. A good idea still depended on a parcel reaching the right address.

Amazon was founded in 1994, and its online store opened in July 1995. Orders arrived from beyond the local area. For customers, the appeal included being able to find a title that a nearby bookshop did not stock. They could search from home rather than call several shops or travel between them.

More than a catalogue

The website gradually became a place to compare products as well as buy them. Customer reviews allowed people to describe what they liked and disliked. A negative review might discourage an immediate sale, but useful information gave a customer a reason to return. Shopping involved reading other people's experiences, not just the description provided by a seller.

Amazon went public in 1997 while it was still building the business. Bezos argued for investment in future growth rather than an emphasis on immediate profit. That approach required shareholders to accept substantial spending and uncertainty. Growing sales alone did not settle the question of whether the company could become financially durable.

The product range expanded beyond books into music, films, toys and electronics. Every new category brought more possibilities for customers and more complications for the company. Products had to be stored, described, located and delivered. A larger catalogue was useful only if the systems behind it could handle the extra work.

In 2000, Amazon Marketplace opened another route to expansion. Other merchants could sell through Amazon's website. This meant the selection could grow without Amazon buying and owning every product itself. A seller gained access to shoppers, while Amazon became the place where those shoppers searched, compared and placed orders.

The arrangement also changed Amazon's relationship with businesses using the site. They were customers of its services and competitors in retail. A merchant could depend on the platform for sales while competing with products Amazon sold directly. The convenience of gathering buyers and sellers in one place came with questions about who controlled that place.

Surviving the crash

The collapse of the internet investment boom in 2000 exposed weaknesses across the technology sector. Investors who had been excited by fast growth started asking more urgent questions about costs and profits. Amazon's share price fell sharply. Its future no longer seemed assured simply because more people were shopping online.

Inside the company, cost cutting meant layoffs and reduced spending. Projects were reconsidered, and operations had to become more efficient. Employees felt the difference between a business trying to expand quickly and one under pressure to show that it could survive. Many other internet companies did not make it through this period.

Amazon continued operating, but survival required more than keeping the website online. A retailer needs to know what it has, where it is and when it can deliver it. Warehouses, transport arrangements and software became central to the company's development. Improving these systems could reduce mistakes and make delivery promises more credible.

Fulfilment centres brought large quantities of goods into organised networks. Scanners and software helped track items through storage, packing and shipment. For a customer, the process might look like a few clicks followed by a knock at the door. Behind it were workers, equipment and decisions about where stock should be held.

Speed became something customers could expect rather than an occasional extra. That expectation placed demands on the organisation providing it. A promise made on a product page had to be supported by the people and facilities responsible for the order. The simplicity of the purchase depended on considerable work out of sight.

A subscription, a reader and a cloud

Amazon introduced Prime in 2005. Customers paid a membership fee for benefits that included fast delivery on eligible purchases. Once someone had paid, ordering again could feel easier than comparing the delivery charges at another shop. The subscription encouraged repeated use of the same retailer.

For customers, Prime offered a predictable arrangement. For Amazon, it created a reason to maintain the relationship beyond a single sale. It also made delivery capacity important to the value of the membership. A subscription promising convenience would lose its appeal if orders regularly failed to arrive as expected.

The Kindle, introduced in 2007, returned the company to books in a different form. A reader could buy a digital book and read it on a device instead of waiting for a printed copy. Publishers and booksellers now had to consider a market in which the store, the delivery process and the reading device could all be linked.

Digital books did not eliminate printed books. They added another way to distribute and read them. Amazon's position in that market gave it influence over how readers discovered titles and how publishers reached those readers. The change involved commercial relationships as well as the replacement of paper with a screen for some purchases.

Another important expansion was less visible to shoppers. Amazon Web Services began offering infrastructure services in 2006. Businesses could obtain computing resources and storage through a service rather than buy and manage all the necessary equipment themselves. What a customer needed could change as a project grew.

This became a business serving organisations whose customers might never visit Amazon's shop. A company could use computing services while selling an entirely different product. Amazon had developed an additional source of income from infrastructure, alongside the infrastructure that moved physical goods.

The businesses reinforced the company's broad presence, but they were not identical. Selling a book, delivering groceries and providing computing capacity involved different customers and operational problems. Describing everything as online shopping missed how far Amazon had expanded beyond its original catalogue.

The costs of convenience

Expansion also brought criticism. Merchants questioned the power Amazon held over access to customers. Concerns included how products appeared in search results and whether information obtained through operating the marketplace could give Amazon an advantage as a competing retailer. Amazon disputed accusations that its practices were improper.

These disputes were about more than whether customers liked low prices. A merchant could build a successful business through a platform and still have little control over the conditions of access. Changes to visibility or service terms could affect sales. The platform's responsibility toward businesses depending on it became a matter of public debate.

Workers raised concerns about conditions inside fulfilment centres, including demanding targets and monitoring. A fast delivery promise did not reveal what a shift felt like for the people picking and packing products. Reports about working conditions brought attention to labour behind a service presented to shoppers as almost effortless.

Traditional retailers also faced growing competition. They had to invest in online sales and delivery while continuing to run physical shops. Amazon contributed to that pressure, but the failure of any individual chain could have several causes. Treating every closure as the result of one competitor would hide differences in debt, management and customer demand.

Amazon's purchase of Whole Foods in 2017 added a large physical grocery business. It showed that stores and online operations could be combined in the same organisation. The company also expanded its presence in entertainment and home devices. Its services reached people during activities well beyond placing an order for a book.

Some businesses associated with Bezos remained separate from Amazon. His purchase of The Washington Post was personal, through a private company. Blue Origin was his separate space venture. Keeping that distinction matters when considering what Amazon owns and what belongs to its founder's other activities.

A different role for the founder

In 2020, Bezos appeared before Congress as lawmakers examined the power of large technology companies. The hearing brought questions about marketplace practices and competition into public view. The company had become large enough that its decisions affected many businesses outside its own workforce.

In February 2021, Amazon announced that Andy Jassy would become chief executive later that year. Jassy had led Amazon Web Services. Bezos moved into the role of executive chair in July. He stepped away from running daily operations, but he did not disappear from the company or give up every formal responsibility.

The change gave him more time for other interests, including Blue Origin. His enthusiasm for space had been part of his life long before Amazon. By then, however, the retailer was an organisation with its own employees, systems and customers. Its operation could no longer be understood only as the decisions of one founder.

The distance between the first online book orders and that leadership transition was considerable. Amazon had learned to manage physical deliveries, attract independent sellers and provide digital services to other organisations. Customers encountered different parts of it without necessarily seeing how those parts connected.

A parcel arriving quickly can be useful. A business renting computing resources can avoid buying equipment before it needs it. Those benefits coexist with questions about competition, working conditions and dependence on powerful intermediaries. Understanding Amazon means looking at both the service visible on a screen and the arrangements that make it possible.
