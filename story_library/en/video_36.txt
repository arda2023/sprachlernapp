After the cartel leader

The operation in Tapalpa

On 22 February 2026, Mexican authorities announced that they had finally reached the man they had pursued for years. Nemesio Rubén Oseguera Cervantes, better known as El Mencho, had been found in Tapalpa, in the western state of Jalisco. He led the Cártel Jalisco Nueva Generación, usually shortened to CJNG.

According to Mexico's defence ministry, troops met armed resistance during an operation intended to capture him. Several people were killed. El Mencho was wounded and died while being transported by air. The first announcement still left formal identification to forensic specialists. Days later, the authorities released his body to relatives after genetic checks.

The news seemed to offer a clear ending. A powerful criminal leader had escaped arrest for years, and now he was dead. But the violence that followed made that ending much less convincing. Vehicles burned, roads were blocked and people across several states faced sudden danger. A leader was gone. The organization associated with his name could still act.

To understand that apparent contradiction, it helps to look beyond the hunt for one man. CJNG had grown through relationships with local groups, access to illegal markets and the ability to frighten people into obedience. Those relationships did not all depend on El Mencho being physically present. His death raised an urgent question about who would take charge, but also a harder one about what would happen to the people living under the cartel's influence.

From an older organization

CJNG did not appear in an empty criminal market. It emerged around 2010 and 2011 from the remains of the Milenio cartel, an organization that had been associated with Sinaloa. Arrests and internal divisions had weakened the older group. Members reorganized, formed new alliances and fought over what remained.

The dates vary slightly between accounts because the creation of a cartel is rarely a single event. There is no public opening ceremony or reliable register of its first members. A name may become visible after the people behind it have already been working together. What matters here is the continuity. CJNG inherited connections and experience from an existing criminal world.

Under El Mencho, it became an independent force and eventually a major rival to Sinaloa. Jalisco gave the group its name, but its reach expanded beyond that state. Researchers at ACLED have documented the growth of CJNG and affiliated groups across central and western Mexico, along with violent struggles over territory.

Maps of cartel influence can make this look simpler than it is. A coloured area might suggest that one organization controls every road, town and business within it. In practice, the presence of an armed group can vary from one place to another. Rivals may operate nearby, local alliances may change, and residents may face pressure from more than one group. A map records an assessment of influence, not a stable border between countries.

That distinction also matters when describing the cartel's size. A large network can be dangerous without being perfectly united. Its members may share an enemy or a source of income while disagreeing about who should lead them. Expansion creates opportunities for a criminal organization, but it also brings people whose loyalty may depend on circumstances.

A name with many branches

Analysts sometimes describe CJNG as a franchise. The comparison refers to smaller groups joining a wider organization while continuing to operate locally. It can help explain how a cartel spreads without sending its central leadership to manage every town.

It should not be taken too literally. A criminal network is not a chain of ordinary shops with a public contract and identical rules. Local commanders have their own contacts, interests and histories. Relationships can involve cooperation, coercion or both. The same name does not prove that every action has been ordered personally by one leader.

For residents, however, uncertainty about the command structure offers little comfort. A threat can still change how someone travels, works or spends money. A business owner does not need to know the identity of every superior in the organization to fear the person making demands at the door. The pressure is immediate even when the wider network is difficult to see.

This is why the language of business success can become misleading. Describing a cartel as efficient or innovative may explain part of its organization, but it can also make violence sound like a management technique with no victims. Its income depends on crimes, and its influence can depend on people believing that refusal will bring punishment.

The central leader still matters. He may settle disputes, maintain relationships and give the organization a recognizable identity. But those functions are different from personally controlling every transaction. The more work is carried out through regional figures and associates, the more there is to investigate beyond the person whose photograph appears on a wanted poster.

More than one source of money

Drug trafficking was central to CJNG's rise. Methamphetamine became an important part of its business, alongside cocaine and later illicit fentanyl. The markets reached beyond Mexico. The damage did too, extending to people who used the drugs and to families dealing with dependence, illness and death.

The cartel also developed sources of income outside the drug trade. ACLED has described activities including extortion, kidnapping and fuel theft. These businesses connect organized crime to people who may have no involvement with drugs at all. Someone can be affected because of where they live, what they grow or the business they run.

One example attracted an official warning in July 2024. FinCEN, the FBI and the US Treasury's sanctions office highlighted fraud associated with Mexican criminal organizations, including CJNG, targeting American owners of timeshares in Mexico. A timeshare gives someone the right to use holiday accommodation for an agreed period. The warning concerned fraud against owners, rather than simply a bad holiday rental.

The distinction matters because it shows how misleading the familiar image of a drug cartel can be. Victims may encounter an apparently commercial proposal and never meet an armed person. The crime can reach across a border through a financial relationship. Its connection to a violent organization may only become clear later.

Having several sources of money can also make an organization harder to dismantle. Interrupting one business does not automatically remove the others. This does not mean every cartel activity succeeds or that enforcement is pointless. It means investigators need to establish how particular people and businesses are connected, rather than assume that one seizure has stopped the whole network.

Fear and public image

The documentary behind this story describes CJNG presenting itself as a protector while using threats and violence. Such a claim deserves scrutiny. A group can denounce a rival's crimes without giving up its own. Public displays of generosity do not establish that the people watching are free to reject the giver.

The same problem applies to images circulated online. A recording can show armed men, expensive possessions or a crowd gathered at an event. It cannot, by itself, show how freely everyone participated or how representative the scene is. Treating a cartel's publicity as a transparent account of local life gives the organization too much control over the story.

Some of the clearest evidence of CJNG's violence came through criminal proceedings. On 1 May 2015, its members shot down a Mexican armed forces helicopter. At least nine people aboard died. In a later US prosecution, evidence attributed the order to El Mencho's son, Rubén Oseguera González, known as El Menchito.

The similar nicknames can cause confusion, but father and son were separate people with separate responsibilities. In September 2024, a federal jury convicted El Menchito of drug trafficking conspiracy and firearms offences. In March 2025, he received a sentence of life imprisonment plus thirty years. The account of the helicopter attack formed part of the evidence described by the US Justice Department.

That record is more useful than an unsupported claim that one cartel caused a precise share of all killings in Mexico. A national homicide total cannot simply be assigned to a single organization. Serious crimes do not become less serious when a writer refuses to attach an unverified number to them. The people who died in the helicopter were enough reason to investigate and prosecute those responsible.

What an operation can achieve

In March 2020, US authorities announced the results of Project Python, an operation targeting CJNG. Over six months, it had produced more than six hundred arrests and three hundred and fifty indictments. Those were different measures. An arrest was not a conviction, and an indictment was a formal accusation that still had to be tested through the legal process.

The figures showed a substantial enforcement effort. They did not establish that the cartel had disappeared. When an organization continues to operate after arrests, it is tempting to call the whole operation a failure. That conclusion asks too much of a single headline. Removing particular offenders and building cases against them can matter even when others remain active.

The opposite conclusion would be premature as well. A large number of arrests does not prove that residents are safer or that an organization has lost the ability to replace people. Assessing the outcome requires looking at what happens afterwards, including whether criminal activity moves, whether new leaders emerge and whether threats against communities decline.

There is a question of whose experience gets counted. An official report may describe the number of suspects detained, while a resident wants to know whether it is safe to report a threat. Both concern the same operation, but they measure different things. A convincing account of progress needs evidence about the second question as well as the first.

CJNG's own origins illustrate the difficulty. It emerged after an older organization had weakened and divided. That history does not mean authorities should leave violent leaders alone. It does show why capturing a leader and preventing a successor organization are different tasks. Courts can punish an individual. Sustained protection for communities requires work beyond that individual's case.

International measures added another layer. In February 2025, the United States designated CJNG a foreign terrorist organization. This was a US legal designation with specific consequences, not a finding that every allegation about everyone linked to the cartel had been proved. Criminal responsibility still required attention to particular people, conduct and evidence.

After El Mencho

The February 2026 operation brought years of pursuit to a close. Mexican authorities said American information had contributed to the effort. That account described a Mexican operation with supplementary US intelligence, rather than an American ground assault. The distinction was important amid debate about how far the United States might intervene against cartels.

The retaliatory violence then exposed the gap between removing a leader and securing the places affected by his organization. For someone facing a blocked road or a burning vehicle nearby, the immediate issue was getting home safely. A major announcement from the government could coexist with fear in the streets.

It was too early in the first days to know how leadership would settle. Later information changed that picture. On 23 July 2026, the US Treasury identified Juan Carlos Gonzalez, also known as Juan Carlos Valencia Gonzalez and Pelon, as CJNG's new leader. Treasury described him as El Mencho's stepson and a longtime member of the organization.

This was a dated assessment by US authorities. It gave a more current answer than simply saying there was no known successor, while leaving room for uncertainty about how authority worked across different local groups. Identifying a leader is not the same as observing every relationship inside a secretive organization.

The history of CJNG leaves no neat final scene. El Mencho's death was a major event, and the prosecution of his son brought a serious criminal case to judgment. Neither event erased the connections through which the organization earned money and exercised force. Those connections remained the subject of investigations and sanctions.

For the people living with cartel violence, the useful measure of change is less dramatic than the fall of a famous boss. It is whether a shop can stay open without threats, whether a journey can be made safely and whether a witness can speak without fearing retaliation. Those are ordinary expectations. Making them dependable is the unfinished work that remains after the cameras leave.
