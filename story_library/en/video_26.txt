The letters that spread fear

An illness in Florida

Robert Stevens worked with pictures at the Sun, a newspaper based in Boca Raton, Florida. In late September 2001, he became ill. The documentary follows his journey from ordinary office work and a family visit to a hospital, where doctors encountered something they had little reason to expect.

Tests identified anthrax, a rare and potentially deadly bacterial disease. Stevens died on 5 October. Another employee at the same media company, Ernesto Blanco, also became seriously ill. He survived after a long hospital stay. Their cases raised a frightening question. How had a disease usually associated with animals and particular occupational exposures reached a newspaper office?

Only weeks had passed since the attacks of 11 September. Americans were still trying to understand what had happened in New York and Washington. Now there was another threat, one that arrived without the noise or visible destruction of a plane crash. People could carry on with an ordinary working day before discovering that something dangerous had reached their workplace.

Stevens was the first person to die in the attacks, but he would not be the last. Public health investigators eventually counted twenty two cases associated with the mailings, including five deaths. Some of those affected handled mail for a living. Others worked in places that received it. The danger extended well beyond the names written on envelopes.

The mail becomes a clue

The investigation gradually connected illnesses to letters sent to media organisations. One was addressed to the NBC news presenter Tom Brokaw. His assistant developed a form of anthrax affecting the skin. A contaminated letter recovered at the organisation became important physical evidence.

The documentary describes scientists and investigators examining suspicious mail while doctors tried to understand new cases. At first, each workplace had only part of the picture. Bringing those reports together showed that the illness in Florida was not simply an isolated medical mystery.

Further letters were sent to senators Tom Daschle and Patrick Leahy. The Daschle letter was opened on 15 October. The Leahy letter was recovered later during the investigation. Discovering contamination or identifying people who had been exposed did not mean that every one of them became ill. Health officials had to establish who needed protection and care while the criminal investigation continued.

The postal system connected offices that otherwise had little in common. Letters passed through sorting facilities and through the hands of workers who were not their intended recipients. Two postal employees died. A hospital worker in New York and an elderly woman in Connecticut also died, showing that the consequences reached beyond the targeted offices.

For people watching the news, that uncertainty was part of the fear. Opening the mail had been a routine act. Suddenly it carried a question that most people had never needed to ask. Could an ordinary envelope be dangerous? Closing affected workplaces and investigating possible contamination disrupted daily life as well as work.

Understanding the illness

Anthrax is caused by bacteria. It occurs naturally and commonly affects animals. Human illness can follow exposure to the organism, including in unusual events such as these attacks. It does not spread between people in the manner of a cold or influenza.

That distinction mattered. The danger did not come from every person who happened to be standing near someone who was sick. Investigators had to identify the places and materials associated with exposure. Doctors also had to recognise an unfamiliar illness quickly enough to treat it. Treatment offered hope, but the deaths showed that recovery could not be assumed.

The attacks were not the first deliberate biological attack in the United States. An earlier event in Oregon in 1984 had caused hundreds of illnesses. Calling the 2001 letters the first would erase that history. What made the letters so unsettling was their arrival during an existing national crisis and their intrusion into familiar workplaces.

The timing encouraged suspicion of a link with the September attacks. But a close sequence of events is not proof of a common organiser. Al Qaeda, responsible for September 11, must not be confused with the later organisation known as Islamic State. The anthrax investigation eventually focused on a very different setting, inside American biological research.

A long search

The investigation became known as Amerithrax. It brought together criminal investigators, postal inspectors, public health specialists and scientists. The documentary follows Scott Decker, an investigator with scientific training, as the work moved from responding to an emergency toward trying to identify whoever had sent the letters.

There was no simple trail from an envelope to a person. Investigators examined the recovered material, compared information from different locations and pursued people who might have relevant knowledge or access. A scientific lead could narrow a question without answering it completely. Someone's professional expertise was not, by itself, evidence that they had committed a crime.

Stephen Hatfill became a prominent focus of suspicion. He had worked in biological defence, and searches of his home attracted intense media attention. Being publicly described as a person of interest placed him under scrutiny long before any court had established wrongdoing.

The Justice Department ultimately ruled him out as the sender. He pursued a lawsuit concerning the handling of personal information and reached a settlement without an admission of liability by the government. His experience exposed another kind of damage an investigation could cause. Public suspicion could overwhelm a person's life even when the authorities eventually concluded that he was not the attacker.

Attention turns to Ivins

Investigators later focused on Bruce Ivins, a scientist who researched anthrax and vaccines at the Army's institute in Fort Detrick, Maryland. He was familiar with the material being studied and had contributed expertise during the inquiry. Investigators came to believe that someone working within that research environment had sent the letters.

Genetic comparisons formed an important part of their account. They identified similarities between the material in the letters and material maintained at the institute. Yet a relationship between samples is not the same as identifying the person who mailed an envelope. Access, opportunity and individual responsibility remained separate questions.

The authorities combined scientific findings with other information about Ivins's activities and behaviour. They also proposed an explanation involving his work on vaccines. That was an investigative theory about motive, not direct knowledge of what he had been thinking. A disturbing personal detail or a mental health difficulty could not establish responsibility on its own.

Ivins died in July 2008 before charges could be filed. There was no criminal trial in which his defence could challenge the prosecution's case, and no conviction. Former colleagues and representatives of his family disputed the allegation that he had carried out the attacks.

In February 2010, the authorities formally closed the investigation, concluding that Ivins had acted alone. That is the official investigative conclusion. It should not be described as a verdict reached after a trial, and his death should not be treated as proof that the conclusion was correct.

What the science could establish

An independent scientific review published in 2011 examined the methods and conclusions used in the inquiry. The National Research Council found that the scientific evidence alone did not permit a definitive conclusion about the origin of the material in the letters. Genetic similarities were consistent with the FBI's account, but other possible sources had not been ruled out.

The review did not examine every part of the criminal case, and it did not establish an alternative perpetrator. Its narrower conclusion still mattered. Scientific evidence needed to be described with its limits intact, especially when the accused person had never stood trial.

For the families of those who died, the attacks were more than an argument about evidence. Five people had been lost, and many others had endured illness, fear or disrupted lives. The survivors' experiences remained real regardless of disagreements about the investigation's final conclusions.

The story began with people doing ordinary jobs, handling photographs, sorting letters and answering office mail. It ended with a vast inquiry that produced an official answer but never a trial of the man investigators identified. Remembering both parts makes the history harder to simplify, and more faithful to what happened.
