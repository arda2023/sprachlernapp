The state sells cigarettes

A company behind the counter

A cigarette packet usually carries a brand name, not a diagram of the institutions behind it. A customer sees a colour, a logo and a familiar product. In China, many apparently separate cigarette brands lead back to the same enormous business: China National Tobacco Corporation, often called China Tobacco.

Outside its home market, the name attracts less attention than many international consumer brands. Yet the company occupies a central position in a country with a vast cigarette market. Its importance goes beyond factories and sales. It is closely tied to the state institutions that oversee the industry, while tobacco generates substantial public revenue.

That arrangement creates the central problem explored by the documentary. A government has a responsibility to protect health. It also receives money from products that cause serious disease. When commercial and regulatory roles are closely connected, a policy that protects people can threaten an important source of income.

The tension does not mean every official has the same priorities. Health workers, researchers and people trying to stop smoking have their own interests and experiences. It does mean that the debate takes place within an unequal structure, where the producer has influence far beyond an ordinary shop counter.

Machines and a larger market

The history begins well before the modern Chinese monopoly. In the nineteenth century, cigarette production depended heavily on workers rolling tobacco by hand. Making more cigarettes meant employing more skilled workers. Machinery changed the scale of the business.

In 1884, the Duke company installed Bonsack cigarette machines at its factory in Durham, North Carolina. The equipment was imperfect, and mechanic William T. O'Brien helped improve it. Once the machines worked more reliably, production costs fell. James B. Duke combined the new manufacturing capacity with aggressive advertising.

This was not merely a faster way to make an existing product. Cheap mass production gave manufacturers a reason to seek much larger markets. Advertising and distribution became crucial companions to the machine. Producing enormous quantities mattered commercially only if people could be persuaded to buy them.

The documentary follows that expansion towards China. Foreign tobacco interests became powerful there before the Communist victory in 1949. During the following years, cigarette factories came under state control. The ownership changed, but the product had already become established in everyday life.

In the early 1980s, the national tobacco corporation brought the state's tobacco interests into a more coordinated structure. The result was a business with reach across production and distribution. Its scale came from a large domestic market and from the institutional arrangements that protected its position within that market.

Two roles under one roof

China Tobacco's relationship with the State Tobacco Monopoly Administration is essential to understanding the system. The commercial corporation and the monopoly administration have closely connected leadership and operations. The administration's responsibilities include overseeing parts of the tobacco supply chain. It is not an independent health authority standing outside the business it regulates.

This creates practical questions. Who decides which interests receive priority when a proposed restriction might reduce sales? How much influence should a producer have over rules governing its products? Can officials responsible for commercial performance also be expected to welcome measures that make smoking less attractive?

The documentary describes the industry as extending from tobacco cultivation through processing and cigarette manufacture to sales and trade. The connections matter because a proposed change does not affect only one factory. It can touch local employment, suppliers, retailers and public budgets. Those relationships can make reform politically difficult even when the health evidence is clear.

The money is substantial, but accounting language matters. Taxes, company profits and contributions to public finances are related quantities, not interchangeable ones. Adding taxes and profits together and comparing the result with another company's profit alone does not produce a fair comparison. Nor does a large fiscal contribution prove that smoking creates a net benefit for society.

Illness has costs that do not appear beside cigarette receipts. A family can lose earnings when someone becomes too ill to work. Treatment takes time and money. Premature death changes households in ways that a revenue total cannot describe. The public debate needs to consider those costs as well as the money collected from sales.

Putting a number on the harm

The documentary draws on reporting by The Examination, which published a striking estimate in May 2026. Its analysis attributed about 57 million deaths between 1990 and 2023 to China Tobacco's products. That figure was a calculation based on mortality modelling and market share, not a list of individually verified deaths caused by one company.

The analysis used estimates from the Global Burden of Disease project, led by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation. It took a lower estimate of tobacco related deaths in China, including harm from other people's smoke, and applied the company's dominant market share. It also discussed uncertainty and criticism of the underlying mortality estimates.

The distinction does not make tobacco harmless. It explains what the number can and cannot establish. A model can reveal the likely scale of a public health burden without identifying the manufacturer responsible for every individual illness. The corporate attribution is the news organisation's analysis, not a direct WHO finding or a judicial verdict.

Long delays also complicate the picture. The cigarette sold today and a diagnosis made today do not necessarily belong to the same period of exposure. Diseases can develop after years of smoking. A company can continue to be associated with severe harm even while smoking becomes less common among younger people.

The point of examining the producer is not to blame people who have become dependent on nicotine. It is to ask who designs, distributes and promotes the product, who earns money from it, and who has the power to change the conditions in which it is sold.

A promise and its implementation

China ratified the WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control in October 2005. It entered into force for China in January 2006. The country is the treaty party, not the tobacco corporation. That distinction matters because the responsibility to implement public health commitments belongs to the state.

The treaty addresses areas such as exposure to tobacco smoke, advertising and information on packaging. Its obligations and accompanying guidance should not be collapsed into the claim that every country must use an identical packet design. The broader issue is whether measures give people meaningful protection and clear information.

Investigative reporting has described resistance to reform. In a 2023 investigation, The Examination reported that China Tobacco had influenced the implementation of tobacco controls, including local rules. It described a 2020 smoking law in Chongqing that allowed designated smoking areas in several types of venues after company leaders met local officials. The reporting attributed influence to the monopoly, rather than establishing a criminal judgment.

The same investigation reported that the company had worked to weaken treaty language and policy measures. China Tobacco and the Chinese government did not respond to the reporters' questions, according to the publication. Their silence is not an admission. The findings remain attributed reporting, while the underlying institutional conflict can be examined in its own right.

The packet as a message

Packaging is a small object with a large communication role. Colours, symbols and the arrangement of a brand name can suggest quality, tradition or status. The documentary describes cigarettes given as gifts and offered during social occasions. These practices exist, but they do not mean every person in China welcomes a cigarette or regards refusal as rude.

A health warning competes with those messages. A brief general sentence may communicate less than a clear explanation of a specific harm. Large warnings and restrictions on branding are intended to reduce the ability of a packet to advertise the product. Their effect should be assessed through evidence, rather than a dramatic claim that one design change alone made an exact number of people quit.

Another message can appear scientific: a lower tar figure. Someone worried about health may interpret it as evidence that one cigarette is safer than another. But the number can come from a smoking machine, which does not behave like a human being. It is not a reliable measure of what an individual will inhale.

The National Cancer Institute explains that cigarettes marketed as light or low tar are not safer than ordinary cigarettes. Changes in how a person smokes can undermine the apparent reduction measured by a machine. A reassuring description on a packet does not remove the risks of cancer and other disease.

This is where information and commercial incentives meet. A customer may want reassurance rather than a reason to stop. A producer may benefit when concern leads to switching brands instead of leaving the market. Public health communication needs to address that misunderstanding without treating the customer as foolish for believing a seemingly technical claim.

Change is already happening

The original documentary makes the sweeping claim that smoking has declined almost everywhere except China. More recent national survey evidence does not support that wording. China's survey found current smoking among people aged fifteen and older fell from 26.6 percent in 2018 to 23.2 percent in 2024.

The decline is real progress, although the remaining burden is large. The same 2024 survey found a substantial difference between men and women. A single national average therefore conceals very different patterns. It also should not be confused with the percentage of the entire population, including young children.

Falling prevalence and continued commercial power can exist together. So can fewer smokers and serious illness caused by past exposure. These are different measurements. Understanding them prevents a false choice between declaring the problem solved and insisting that nothing has improved.

State ownership alone does not explain every country's smoking pattern. Private tobacco companies have also resisted restrictions and promoted misleading claims. The relevant questions concern the strength of health policy, the independence of decisions, commercial influence and access to help for people who want to stop.

The future is not fixed by the size of the company. Institutions can change priorities, clearer warnings can challenge familiar assumptions, and fewer young people can begin smoking. None of that happens automatically. It requires decisions that place preventable illness beside revenue when judging the industry's role.

At the shop counter, the product still looks small. A packet fits in a pocket and a purchase takes only a moment. Behind it stand factories, public institutions and years of health consequences. Seeing those connections makes the story larger than a choice between brands. It becomes a question of what a state is prepared to protect when the product it sells harms the people it serves.
