Selling the dream

Learning to sell

Jordan Belfort discovered early that he liked selling things. As a teenager, he sold Italian ices and earned enough money to take his ambitions seriously. He wanted a comfortable life, but comfort alone would never have satisfied him. He wanted the sort of wealth other people stopped to look at.

He briefly considered dentistry. The prospect of years of training for an ordinary professional income did not appeal to him, so he left. A business selling meat and seafood seemed more promising. He could knock on a door, start a conversation and persuade someone to buy something they had not planned to buy that morning.

Orders came in, but delivering them was harder. Food spoiled, bills went unpaid and customers did not always receive what they expected. The business failed. Belfort had found that he could sell. He had also found that enthusiasm on a doorstep could not keep a badly organised company running.

His next opportunity was in finance. At the brokerage firm L. F. Rothschild, he learned how brokers talked to clients and how they presented investments. He watched the confidence of people who seemed completely at home around large sums of money. Then the stock market crashed in October 1987, and he lost his job.

Afterward, Belfort began selling penny stocks. These were cheap shares in small companies, often difficult to sell again and far riskier than his customers understood. What interested him most was the commission. A broker could keep a large part of the money from a sale. For someone good on the telephone, that made each successful call unusually rewarding.

The customer did not need to understand the company in detail for Belfort to make a sale. He could describe an exciting future, suggest that other investors would soon discover the opportunity and make waiting sound like a mistake. Questions about the business became interruptions to a conversation about getting rich.

Inside Stratton Oakmont

With a partner, Belfort built Stratton Oakmont. The name suggested an established institution. The business grew from an ordinary office filled with people making telephone calls. Many recruits had little experience of financial markets. Some had worked in bars or left college. Their most useful qualification was a willingness to keep selling after a stranger said no.

Belfort taught them how to control a conversation. A hesitant answer from a potential customer was a problem to overcome. The broker would return to the possible profit, introduce more urgency and steer the discussion toward a purchase. The method could be taught and repeated, which allowed the firm to grow beyond Belfort's own calls.

People answering those calls often had modest savings and little experience of investing. They heard someone who sounded informed and certain. A retirement fund, money saved for children or the proceeds of a loan could become the basis of a purchase. The broker's confidence made a risky decision feel considered.

The firm also manipulated share prices. It obtained shares cheaply, then used its sales force to encourage customers to buy them. As demand increased, the price rose. That rise helped the sales pitch because brokers could point to a stock that appeared to be doing well. Those controlling the shares could then sell their holdings at the higher price.

Customers who bought near the top were left with the losses when the price fell. The money had already moved. A company did not need to become more productive or sell a better product for the people running this scheme to profit. They needed other people to keep buying until they had sold their own shares.

Within the office, successful brokers received bonuses, expensive gifts and attention. Those who performed badly could be humiliated or dismissed. The pressure to sell affected what employees were willing to ask. Doubts about a company were inconvenient when a manager wanted another completed order.

Belfort's own spending became part of the attraction. He bought expensive cars, houses and a yacht. Employees could see the rewards he promised. The parties and widespread drug use made the office increasingly difficult to separate from the life he lived outside it. People were encouraged to admire the excess while overlooking where the money came from.

Complaints and records

As Stratton Oakmont expanded, investors began complaining. Some had watched shares lose most of their value after being urged to buy them. Regulators examined suspicious activity. Employees who questioned what they were selling faced pressure to carry on.

Hiding the proceeds created further problems. Money moved through companies and accounts overseas. Accounts held under other names made ownership harder to follow. Belfort treated these arrangements as protection, but investigators were interested in the route the money had taken and why it needed to be concealed.

His private life was also deteriorating. Drug use damaged his relationships and his judgment. Arguments at home accompanied the pressure building around the business. His wealth allowed him to keep spending, but it did not make the consequences disappear.

The loss of his yacht in 1996 became one of the best known episodes of that period. Belfort insisted on a voyage in dangerous weather despite warnings from the captain. The boat took on water and the people aboard had to be rescued. It was an expensive disaster that could have ended much worse.

For investigators, bank records mattered more than the spectacle. FBI agent Gregory Coleman worked through evidence of Belfort's financial dealings. Transactions could be compared with reported income, property purchases and the accounts given by people who knew the firm. A telephone promise might vanish as soon as a call ended. The movement of money left a record.

Former employees helped the investigation. Some faced legal trouble of their own. Others had reasons to stop protecting their employer. Recordings and documents made it harder for Belfort to control the story. People who had once depended on the business were now describing how it worked.

The investigation eventually reached Belfort himself. He faced serious fraud and money laundering charges. Rather than continue defending everyone around him, he cooperated with the authorities. Information about colleagues and associates became part of his effort to reduce the consequences he faced. Employees who had learned the business from him also faced prosecution.

Another way to make money

Belfort served twenty two months in prison. During that time, he began turning his experiences into a story. Fellow prisoner Tommy Chong encouraged him to write about what had happened. There was plenty of material: the sales calls, the money, the drugs and the effort to keep everything from collapsing.

His memoir, The Wolf of Wall Street, appeared in 2007. Readers encountered the events through the voice of the man at their centre. The excesses that had surrounded the fraud became scenes people wanted to read. A second book followed, and the story eventually reached Hollywood.

In 2013, Martin Scorsese's film starring Leonardo DiCaprio brought Belfort's name to a much larger audience. Viewers watched lavish parties and reckless behaviour, often presented with humour. The film made the world of Stratton Oakmont vivid, but the attention also helped turn Belfort into a celebrity.

For the investors, the aftermath was much less entertaining. Losing savings can mean delaying retirement, taking on debt or abandoning plans that took years to finance. A film deal for the person responsible does not replace that money. Nor does it return the time people spent trying to recover their losses.

Belfort was ordered to pay about 110 million dollars in restitution. The order required him to repay the people harmed by the fraud. The size of that obligation helps explain why his later public career attracts criticism. Money earned from telling the story exists alongside money owed to the people harmed by it.

He went on to work as a speaker and sales trainer. The ability to hold an audience, which had helped him sell shares, could also help him sell advice. His reputation made people curious enough to listen, even when that reputation came from a conviction for fraud.

There is a practical question for anyone listening to a persuasive salesperson: what can be checked independently? A confident voice does not explain a company's finances. An exciting account of future profits does not make those profits certain. At Stratton Oakmont, customers made decisions without receiving an honest account of the risks or of the broker's interests.

The firm's growth depended on those decisions being repeated, call after call. Its collapse ended the operation, but the losses remained with the people who had trusted it. Belfort gained another audience through books, film and public appearances. His former customers were still dealing with what happened after they put down the telephone.
