The price after checkout

A meal costs twenty euros. At checkout, a payment option draws attention to a smaller amount due today. The food arrives, the evening passes and the remaining payments stay on the calendar. Nothing has made the meal cheaper. The timing has changed, and that difference is the starting point for understanding purchases paid in instalments.

Splitting a payment can be convenient. It can also make a purchase feel smaller than it is. One agreement may be easy to remember. Several agreements from different shops can create a schedule that is harder to follow. The important amount is not only today's payment, but everything still owed after the order is complete.

A payment company becomes a bank

Klarna was founded in Sweden in 2005. Its business developed around making payments easier for customers and merchants, including ways to pay after ordering. It acquired Sofort in 2014 and obtained a full banking licence in 2017. These milestones describe different stages of development, not a sudden transformation into a bank as large as Europe's biggest lenders.

Payment services and lending overlap, but they are not identical. Moving money from a customer's account to a merchant is different from giving the customer time to repay. A company may offer both. Describing every transaction through that company as a new consumer loan would obscure how its products actually differ.

The same distinction matters when comparing companies. Transaction volume measures purchases processed over a period. An outstanding loan balance measures money still owed at a particular time. Revenue is another figure again. Comparing them without checking their definitions can make growth look more dramatic, or more alarming, than the underlying accounts justify.

What the small payment leaves out

Deferred payment products do not all have the same terms. Some divide a purchase into a few payments without interest if the conditions are met. Others are longer financing arrangements that may charge interest. The available products, repayment schedules and charges vary by market and contract. One quoted annual rate cannot describe every customer's offer.

For the shopper, the attraction is immediate access without paying the full amount immediately. For the merchant, offering another payment option may help complete a sale. The provider earns money through its business arrangements, which can include merchant fees and, for some products, interest or other charges. A product without customer interest is not necessarily a service with no revenue.

The risk becomes easier to see when purchases overlap. A household might have payments for clothes, a household appliance and several smaller orders due in the same week. Each checkout showed a manageable first payment. Together, the remaining obligations may compete with rent, transport and ordinary food spending.

This does not mean every user is irresponsible or that a small purchase inevitably leads to insolvency. People use credit for different reasons and with different resources. Illness, irregular income or an unexpected bill can disrupt a schedule that previously seemed manageable. A useful explanation examines those mechanisms without turning borrowers into a stereotype.

What credit checks can tell us

A lender may examine information before approving a purchase. That can include its own repayment history and information from credit reporting agencies. Checks and reporting practices differ across products and countries. The effect of an application on a credit score is also different from the consequences of missed payments.

It would therefore be misleading to say every Klarna purchase harms a German credit score, or that every check only searches for unpaid bills. Those claims collapse different procedures into one rule. The relevant description is the one for the particular product and market, including how repayment information is handled.

Overlapping borrowing has attracted research attention. A study published by the United States Consumer Financial Protection Bureau in January 2025 examined data linking instalment loans from six providers with credit records. It investigated repeated use and loans held at the same time. That kind of evidence is more informative than a collection of social media posts boasting about unpaid purchases.

Losses on the other side

When customers do not repay, the provider faces credit risk. But a figure labelled provision for credit losses is not simply a bag of money permanently lost during that quarter. Klarna's reporting definition includes provisions for future losses as well as realised losses. Expected losses and money ultimately recovered need careful interpretation.

Multiplying one quarter's provision by four can produce an arithmetic illustration. It cannot establish what the annual result will be. Lending volumes, product mix, economic conditions and repayment behaviour may change. A higher absolute provision also needs context, including the size and risk of the lending activity that generated it.

Funding is more complicated than saying the company borrows every payment and hopes for the best. Klarna's reporting includes consumer deposits among its funding sources. Funding carries costs and obligations, while lending creates assets and risks. Understanding the balance requires examining both sides rather than treating all incoming money as company profit.

A claim that a lender is insolvent needs evidence about its financial position, not just a viral rumour or one comparison of short term figures. Liquidity, capital, asset quality and the timing of obligations all matter. This does not make credit losses harmless. It means a serious conclusion requires more than a striking number.

Back at checkout, the basic distinction remains simple. Paying later changes when money leaves an account. It does not erase the price, and approval does not make a repayment schedule disappear. For companies and customers alike, the real story continues after the button is pressed and the purchase feels finished.
