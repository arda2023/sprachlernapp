When the boom ended

Buying a better life

In the United States of the 1920s, a new car or radio could make the future feel close enough to touch. Factories produced goods that had once seemed expensive or unusual. Electricity changed homes and workplaces. Cars created demand for steel, rubber and oil, while roads and petrol stations connected more of the country.

Mass production helped manufacturers make goods more quickly. More people could buy them, especially if they did not have to pay the full price immediately. Instalment plans divided a purchase into smaller payments. A household could take a radio home and pay for it over the following months, provided its income continued.

The prosperity was uneven. Many farmers had expanded during the First World War, when demand for food was high. Afterward, falling agricultural prices made debts harder to repay. Buying machinery could increase output without solving the problem of low prices. A farmer might produce more and still struggle to meet the next payment.

In growing cities, those difficulties were easy to overlook. New buildings, busy shops and popular entertainment offered visible signs of success. Credit allowed spending to run ahead of the money people already had. As long as wages and sales continued, the next payment could seem like a manageable concern for another day.

The same confidence reached the stock market. Rising share prices attracted people who hoped to increase their savings quickly. Some bought shares with borrowed money. They paid part of the purchase price themselves and borrowed the rest, using the shares as security. This was known as buying on margin.

Borrowing increased the possible return on the investor's own money, but it also increased the danger. A fall in the share price reduced the value securing the loan. A lender could demand more money or require the shares to be sold. Someone who had expected to profit from rising prices could instead be forced to sell into a falling market.

During the boom, that possibility received less attention than stories of easy gains. Stock tips passed between friends and strangers. A price that had risen yesterday seemed like evidence that it would rise again tomorrow. Warnings that prices had become detached from the strength of the businesses behind them were unwelcome.

The crash and what followed

In October 1929, selling overwhelmed the market. Black Tuesday, October 29, became one of the best known days of the crash. Investors tried to dispose of shares as prices fell. People who had borrowed to buy them faced demands they could not always meet. Wealth recorded on paper disappeared as buyers withdrew or offered much lower prices.

The crash was a major shock, but the Great Depression was not a single bad day on the stock exchange. Economic activity had already begun to weaken. Over the following years, falling production, banking crises and unemployment reinforced one another. Families who owned no shares could still lose their jobs or their savings.

Banks depended on borrowers repaying loans. When businesses failed and households lost income, more loans went unpaid. Depositors then worried about whether their bank could return their money. If enough people tried to withdraw savings at once, the bank could come under severe pressure.

The banking panics did not all happen immediately after the crash. Waves of failures and withdrawals followed during the early 1930s. A closed bank could leave families unable to access the money they needed for ordinary expenses. It could also leave a local business without the credit it used to pay workers or buy supplies.

An employer losing customers cut production. Reduced production meant fewer hours or fewer jobs. Workers with less income bought less from other businesses. Each decision could seem necessary to the person making it while adding to the problems faced by someone else.

The crisis spread beyond the United States. International loans and trade connected economies that had not fully recovered from the First World War. When American finance became harder to obtain, countries that depended on it faced new pressure. Falling demand and declining trade made recovery more difficult across national borders.

Trying to get through the week

By 1933, roughly a quarter of the American civilian labour force was unemployed, according to historical estimates. Behind that figure were households trying to work out which expenses they could postpone. Rent still had to be paid. Children still needed food and clothes. Finding another job became harder when many neighbours were searching too.

Breadlines and soup kitchens became familiar sights. People who had previously supported themselves needed help to eat. A factory worker could return to the gates repeatedly without knowing whether work would resume. Families delayed plans that once seemed ordinary, including marriage, a home or children.

In parts of the Great Plains, the economic crisis was accompanied by an environmental disaster. Drought struck land made vulnerable by farming practices. Strong winds carried away dry soil. Dust storms damaged crops and filled homes, turning the ground on which families depended into a source of danger.

People left farms and travelled in search of work. California became a destination for many, but reaching another state did not guarantee a job or a decent place to live. Migration exchanged a familiar hardship for an uncertain situation. Families carried what they could and tried to earn enough to stay together.

President Herbert Hoover encouraged voluntary assistance and introduced measures intended to support recovery, but he resisted the scale of direct federal relief many people wanted. Settlements of makeshift shelters became known as Hoovervilles. His name was attached to the visible evidence that families could no longer afford proper housing.

In 1932, veterans of the First World War travelled to Washington to demand early payment of a bonus that was not yet due. They needed money during the crisis and established camps while pressing their demand. The removal of the protesters by the army, including the use of tear gas and the destruction of camps, deepened anger at the government.

Work, relief and new rules

Franklin D. Roosevelt took office in March 1933, amid a severe banking crisis. His administration moved to stabilise the banks and restore confidence. The New Deal brought a range of programmes addressing immediate hardship and the institutions that shaped economic life. They developed over several years rather than arriving as a single finished plan.

The Civilian Conservation Corps employed young men on conservation work, including planting trees. Public employment programmes later hired people to build and repair roads, schools and other facilities. Receiving a wage allowed workers to buy necessities while producing things their communities could use.

The Tennessee Valley Authority developed projects that included dams and electricity generation. Such investment changed the opportunities available in a region where many households had lacked electricity. Recovery meant different things in different places: a wage, a safer bank account or a service that had previously been out of reach.

Financial reforms aimed to reduce the danger of another collapse. Deposit insurance gave savers protection within the scheme's limits. The Securities and Exchange Commission became responsible for overseeing securities markets and enforcing rules. Social Security established a system intended to provide support in old age and other forms of protection.

Artists and writers also found work through federal programmes. Photographs and accounts from the period recorded families on the road, people seeking work and the conditions in which they lived. These records preserved details that national figures could not show, including the effort required to get through an ordinary day.

Recovery was incomplete and uneven. New Deal measures helped many people and changed the role of government, but they did not bring the Depression to a clear end during the 1930s. Another recession in 1937 interrupted the improvement. A family that had recently regained an income could once again face insecurity.

The approach of the Second World War changed demand for American production. Orders and government spending expanded, and factories produced equipment and supplies for war. The return to full employment was bound up with that mobilisation. It was not simply the final step in a smooth recovery from the stock market crash.

The Depression lasted long enough to shape decisions far beyond the years of the crisis. For people who had lost a job, a farm or access to their savings, promises of permanent prosperity could sound different afterward. A radio bought on credit had once represented an affordable future. During the Depression, the next payment could become a problem a family had no way to solve.
