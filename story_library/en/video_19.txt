When the cash machine disappears

The damage left behind

A destroyed cash machine leaves a hole in more than a bank wall. Glass covers the pavement. A building may need repairs before people can return. Customers who used to withdraw money nearby must find somewhere else. Even when thieves leave without cash, the neighbourhood still pays a price.

Explosive attacks on cash machines became a serious concern in Germany during the early 2020s. The Federal Criminal Police Office recorded 496 attempted and completed attacks in 2022. That distinction matters. The figure does not mean that criminals successfully took money on 496 occasions.

The following year brought 461 cases, a fall of about seven percent. It was an improvement, but still the second highest annual total since the statistics began in 2005. These are figures for those years, not proof that attacks have continued to rise ever since.

An empty machine can make an unsuccessful theft look almost harmless in a headline. Explosions, however, can endanger residents, passersby and emergency workers regardless of how much money disappears. Measuring stolen cash alone misses much of the damage.

Why cash still matters

To understand the attraction of cash machines, it helps to look at ordinary shopping. Germany has a long habit of paying with notes and coins. In the Bundesbank's study of payment behaviour in 2023, cash accounted for roughly 51 percent of recorded transactions, though only about 26 percent of their total value.

Those two percentages describe different things. Buying a bread roll and paying for an expensive appliance each count as one transaction. The appliance has much more weight when researchers add up the money spent. A country can therefore make many small cash payments while using electronic methods for a larger share of spending.

People who use cash need places to obtain it. But cash use alone cannot explain attacks. Security, the location of machines and the activities of organised groups also matter. Nor does a comparison between two countries establish which single measure caused their crime figures to change.

Across a border

German investigations have included groups based in the Netherlands, and cooperation between authorities in both countries is important. A national border does not make a suspect unreachable. Investigations, arrests and prosecutions can involve police and prosecutors from several countries.

Describing the Netherlands as a place where offenders can return without consequences misses that cooperation. Nationality and neighbourhood are also poor substitutes for evidence about a particular crime. They do not establish individual guilt or tell us what most people in that community do.

There is a more useful question to ask. When security improves in one place, do offenders stop, change their methods or look elsewhere? Displacement is a possibility that authorities must consider. It is not a reason to describe an entire neighbouring country as a refuge for criminals.

Protecting money and people

One approach tries to remove the reward. Security systems can stain banknotes or bond them together, making stolen cash harder to use. The point is to discourage attacks by reducing the prospect of usable money.

These measures are not a guarantee that an explosion will cause no harm. German authorities have recommended combining protections and assessing the risks at each location. A machine inside an occupied building raises concerns that differ from those at another site. Protecting the cash and protecting the people nearby are related tasks, but they are not identical.

Removing a machine also removes that particular target. For customers, though, it can mean a longer journey to collect cash. That burden is especially noticeable when someone has limited mobility or few alternatives nearby. Simply counting fewer machines does not tell us whether a community has become better served.

Banks and authorities therefore face decisions that extend beyond replacing broken equipment. They must consider security, access to cash and the consequences for surrounding homes and shops. The people who live above a bank should not have to accept danger as part of a convenient service. The people who depend on that service should not disappear from the discussion either.
