The assistant and the secrets

An arrest in Dresden

On 22 April 2024, investigators arrested Jian G. in Dresden. He worked as an assistant to Maximilian Krah, an AfD politician in the European Parliament. The allegations immediately connected an individual criminal investigation with a much wider question: what happens when someone trusted inside a political office passes information to a foreign intelligence service?

At the time of the arrest, the accusations were not a verdict. More than a year later, a court would reach findings about G.'s activities. Keeping those stages separate matters. The case involved evidence, denials and legal proceedings, not simply a suspicious biography that explained itself.

The documentary begins with the apparent contradictions in G.'s life. He had participated in circles critical of the Chinese government. He also worked for a politician who publicly favoured closer relations with China. People who encountered him in one setting did not necessarily know much about his activities in another.

The story is therefore partly about access to documents and partly about access to people. An assistant can become familiar to colleagues. A volunteer can earn trust by reliably helping with ordinary tasks. Neither role looks dramatic from the outside. Both can place a person close to information that others would struggle to obtain.

From a client to an assistant

According to the documentary, G.'s relationship with Krah began with a business problem. G. was involved in importing goods from China and sought legal help. Krah was a lawyer. Their later relationship developed from that earlier professional contact, rather than beginning when G. arrived at the European Parliament.

The documentary describes G. as a quiet person who did not readily discuss his private life. It also includes memories from a woman active in Chinese dissident circles. She recalled him helping with practical arrangements, including collecting guests arriving for events. In her account, he appeared helpful and dependable.

Those recollections explain how trust could develop. They do not independently establish every event in his life or the motives behind each action. A person remembering a reliable volunteer after an arrest may reassess earlier encounters, but a later suspicion does not turn every quiet moment into proof of a secret plan.

For opposition groups living abroad, trust carries particular weight. People may gather to discuss a government they oppose, support visitors or organise public events. Their names, contacts and movements can be sensitive even when no document bears an official secrecy label. Being away from their country of origin does not make those details unimportant.

The documentary says some people later found G.'s behaviour unusual. Such accounts belong to the history of how those around him came to doubt him. The criminal case, however, required evidence of conduct on behalf of an intelligence service. Suspicion within a social circle and a judicial finding are different things.

Inside a parliamentary office

G. began working for an AfD member of the European Parliament in September 2019. His employer was Krah. The role brought him into an institution where elected representatives and their staff discuss legislation, trade and international affairs. According to the documentary, his work included assistance with economic matters and contacts involving China.

An assistant needs information to do that job. Parliamentary work depends on staff being able to prepare meetings, follow discussions and help a member understand complicated material. The ordinary purpose of access is to support that work. The security problem arises when information obtained through the role is used for a different, unauthorised purpose.

The documentary portrays Krah as valuing G.'s knowledge of trade. It also describes Krah's public support for closer relations with China. Such political positions can be criticised or debated, but they do not by themselves establish a crime. Advocating trade, meeting foreign representatives and working for an intelligence service are not interchangeable activities.

That distinction makes the case more demanding than a story in which every contact is suspicious. Investigators had to consider what information was collected, what happened to it and on whose behalf the person acted. The assistant's employer, political party and acquaintances could not simply be assigned the same responsibility by association.

The prosecution's case

In April 2025, federal prosecutors announced charges against G. and a second defendant, identified here as X. They alleged that G. had collected parliamentary information for a Chinese intelligence service. Their public summary also described information about AfD politicians and surveillance of Chinese opposition figures in Germany.

The indictment alleged a long period of intelligence work. The later court announcement did not use exactly the same starting date. That difference is a reason to identify the source of a claim, rather than combining every detail from the investigation and trial into a single seamless account.

The allegations concerning opposition figures were especially significant. A parliamentary document might reveal the direction of a policy discussion. Information about a dissident could reveal a person's identity, relationships or activities. The two kinds of collection could serve different interests while both depending on access gained through trust.

The prosecution also described a separate source of information at Leipzig/Halle airport. X. worked for a company providing logistics services there. Prosecutors alleged that she supplied G. with information about flights, cargo and passengers for onward transmission to Chinese intelligence. The material included information connected with military transport.

These were not details that had to come from a politician's desk. The case linked information from different workplaces and social environments. An assistant's office, a logistics job and a dissident group each offered a different view of people and institutions. The alleged intelligence activity concerned the collection and use of those views.

A judgment in September

On 30 September 2025, the Higher Regional Court in Dresden announced its judgment after thirteen days of trial. It found that G. had worked for a Chinese intelligence service since 2007. The court described his activities as including the identification and monitoring of Chinese opposition figures, collection of information about the AfD and information about European Parliament work.

The court also found that G. and X. had collected information about flights, freight and passengers at Leipzig/Halle and passed it to Chinese intelligence, particularly information about military transports. These were findings attributed to the court, not merely suspicions repeated from the day of the arrest.

G. received a sentence of four years and nine months for intelligence activity in a particularly serious case. X. received one year and nine months, suspended for a probation period of three years. The different sentences reflected separate decisions about the two defendants. They should not be reduced to a vague statement that everyone involved received the same punishment.

The court's announcement also recorded G.'s denial. During the trial, he had stated that he had not worked for a Chinese intelligence service and was innocent. Reporting the judgment accurately includes both what the court found and the position the defendant maintained.

The September judgment could be challenged through a legal appeal. The documentary reports that G. filed one. A later final outcome has not been established for this account, so the September 2025 decision is presented as that court's judgment, without claiming that all avenues of review have ended. The existence of an appeal does not erase a trial decision, and the existence of that decision does not answer what happened in a later review.

The separate questions about Krah

The assistant's conviction raised questions about his employer's judgment and the security of the office. It did not itself establish that Krah knew of or participated in espionage. That required separate evidence. Asking whether a politician had been careless and alleging that he committed a crime are different claims.

In May 2025, reporting based on statements from the Dresden prosecution authorities described a separate investigation into Krah over suspected bribery and money laundering connected with Chinese payments. The allegations concerned his earlier work as a member of the European Parliament. They were allegations under investigation, not a conviction.

Krah denied wrongdoing. He said the payments concerned invoices for legal services and rejected the accusations. His explanation did not settle the investigation, but it belonged in an account of the dispute. Calling the payments proven bribes would go beyond the evidence established here.

On 26 February 2026, the Bundestag approved the execution of judicial search and seizure orders concerning Krah. Parliamentary approval allowed an investigative step. It was not a vote declaring him guilty. Reporting the following day described searches at third parties and said they did not represent a new investigation with new accusations.

These dated steps show that the questions extended beyond the assistant's trial. They do not supply a final outcome for Krah's separate case. The difference between an investigation, an indictment and a conviction is essential when public figures are discussed, especially when political opponents already have strong views about them.

What access can reveal

The documentary treats the affair as a warning about foreign influence in democratic institutions. Its interviewees discuss the possibility that ideological sympathy and political relationships can make some contacts easier to establish. That is an interpretation of risk. It is not a judicial finding that every person with similar views is engaged in espionage.

An intelligence service does not necessarily need one dramatic secret to gain useful information. Repeated observations can help explain priorities, disagreements and likely decisions. In this case, the court's account linked parliamentary work with information about a political party and opposition figures. The concern was broader than a single stolen page.

At the same time, a foreign background is not evidence of disloyalty. G.'s biography matters because it helps explain the settings in which he worked and met people. It does not justify suspicion of Chinese residents, citizens of Chinese origin or dissidents as a group. The people reportedly spied upon included opponents of the Chinese government themselves.

The case also shows why political criticism should remain specific. A party can be asked to explain hiring decisions, access arrangements and its handling of warnings. Those questions need not turn into an assertion that every member committed a criminal offence. Clear distinctions make accountability more useful, not less serious.

After trust is broken

For the people who remembered G. as a helpful participant at events, the case reached beyond parliamentary procedure. Tasks that had seemed ordinary were now part of a troubling history. The documentary's interviews convey that reassessment without giving an outside reader access to every private experience of those involved.

The institutional problem remains practical. Staff need information to work, and political organisations need contact with the outside world. Security cannot mean treating every colleague as an enemy or ending all foreign conversations. It does require attention to what access permits, how concerns are handled and whether responsibility is clear.

The arrest in Dresden led to a trial judgment about one assistant and another defendant. It also prompted scrutiny of a politician and debate about the protection of democratic institutions. Those are connected developments, but they are not the same legal conclusion.

The most revealing part of the story is how ordinary many of its settings were: an office, a business relationship, an event where someone helped arriving guests. Understanding what happened requires more than the atmosphere of a spy thriller. It requires evidence about specific actions, attention to the people affected and the discipline to leave unanswered questions unanswered until the evidence supports an answer.
