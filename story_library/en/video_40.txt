DMT and the feeling of reality

A room that seems to disappear

The person lying in a research room has not physically travelled anywhere. Yet, after receiving DMT, the room may seem to disappear. Colours, spaces and apparent figures can replace familiar surroundings. Some people describe fear, others wonder. A few minutes can feel detached from ordinary time. When the effects fade, explaining what happened can be harder than describing a dream.

DMT is short for dimethyltryptamine. The substance discussed here is usually written more precisely as N,N dimethyltryptamine. It belongs to a group of drugs called psychedelics, which can profoundly alter perception and the sense of self. Calling it the strongest psychedelic in the world would suggest a simple ranking that does not exist. Intensity, duration and physical effects are different questions.

The documentary behind this story introduces a participant it calls Sarah. In its account, frightening patterns give way to playful figures and a feeling of encountering another presence. These are reports of her experience. They do not establish that an independent being entered the room or that she travelled to another universe. Still, the experience felt important to her, and that feeling itself deserves investigation.

This is what makes DMT interesting to researchers. The question is not only why someone sees unusual images. It is also how an experience can acquire such certainty. What makes an imagined place feel real? Why does a perceived figure seem to have intentions? And can studying those changes tell us something about ordinary consciousness?

A plant with a history

Long before laboratory researchers studied DMT, Indigenous communities in the Amazon had knowledge of psychoactive plants and preparations. These practices belonged to particular cultures, with their own meanings and traditions. They were not waiting for a European visitor to make them significant. A botanical description records one kind of knowledge, but it does not replace the knowledge of the people using a plant.

In the nineteenth century, the English botanist Richard Spruce encountered preparations known by names including caapi and ayahuasca during his travels in South America. He collected plant material and described what he observed. The experiences puzzled him. The tools needed to identify all the relevant chemicals were not available to him in the way they are to researchers today.

It is tempting to tell this as a story in which Spruce collected the wrong plant and missed the real ingredient. That would be misleading. The vine now called Banisteriopsis caapi has an important pharmacological role. It contains compounds that affect the breakdown of other substances in the body. It is not simply an inactive container for something found elsewhere.

Ayahuasca and isolated DMT should therefore not be treated as identical. A preparation commonly studied by scientists combines DMT with plant compounds that inhibit enzymes responsible for its breakdown. This interaction can allow DMT to have effects when swallowed. Preparations vary, and their other ingredients also matter. The chemistry helps explain the combination without telling us exactly what was in every historical cup.

The names of related substances require equal care. Another psychedelic often discussed alongside DMT is known as 5 MeO DMT. It is a different compound. An experience or treatment result involving one cannot automatically be assigned to the other. Similar names are not evidence that the substances have identical effects or risks.

Research returns to the laboratory

DMT acts on receptors involved in serotonin signalling, including the serotonin 2A receptor. Serotonin is a chemical messenger with many functions. The fact that a drug affects this system does not reduce its action to a simple change in happiness. Perception, emotion and the processing of information involve interacting systems, not one chemical switch for each feeling.

During the twentieth century, interest in psychedelics spread beyond laboratories into popular culture. The documentary describes Terence McKenna, whose accounts of strange spaces and apparently intelligent figures became influential. His interpretations went beyond describing what he felt. He proposed that these experiences revealed something hidden about reality. That is a philosophical claim, distinct from an observation a laboratory can independently confirm.

Legal restrictions also changed the conditions of research. The United Nations Convention on Psychotropic Substances was adopted in 1971 and entered into force in 1976. It established international controls while recognizing medical and scientific uses. Research became difficult under restrictive systems, but saying that every investigation stopped everywhere would erase the differences between countries, institutions and periods.

In the 1990s, psychiatrist Rick Strassman helped renew controlled human research with DMT in the United States. His published studies measured both bodily responses and participants' descriptions. They reported rapid changes in perception and emotion alongside effects such as increased blood pressure and heart rate. These were supervised experiments with selected volunteers, not demonstrations that the substance was harmless in any setting.

Strassman's popular description of DMT as a spirit molecule captured the character of some accounts. It was a memorable phrase, not a scientific classification showing that a molecule carries a spirit. The words helped frame a public conversation in which laboratory findings, personal meaning and spiritual interpretation often became difficult to separate.

Listening without assuming

Reports of encounters are among the most striking parts of that conversation. People may describe guides, unfamiliar beings or figures that seem to communicate without speech. Some experiences are comforting. Others are frightening or confusing. A researcher can take the report seriously without deciding that its explanation must be literally correct.

A survey published in 2020 gathered responses from 2,561 people about apparent entity encounters after DMT. Participants were asked to recall their most memorable encounter. The design matters: the researchers selected people who had something of this kind to report. The survey could describe those accounts, but it could not establish how often such experiences occur among everyone who uses DMT.

An online account also reaches a researcher through several filters. Someone has to remember the event, choose words for it and decide to share it. The person reading it then has to interpret those words. Two people might use the same label for rather different experiences, or different labels for experiences that overlap. Numbers do not remove those difficulties.

This does not make personal accounts worthless. They reveal what people found vivid, frightening or meaningful. They can help researchers develop better questions. But a collection of reports about apparently independent beings is still a collection of reports. It does not become a test of whether those beings exist outside the experience.

The same distinction applies to changes in belief. Someone may interpret an encounter as spiritual and reconsider earlier views. That tells us about the person's interpretation and its consequences. A retrospective questionnaire cannot by itself show that DMT reliably changes everyone's beliefs, or that the belief adopted afterwards is more accurate than the one held before.

The brain's changing patterns

Modern researchers can compare these descriptions with measurements of brain activity. In a study published in 2023, Christopher Timmermann and colleagues used electrical recordings and functional brain imaging in twenty healthy volunteers. They compared DMT with placebo. The results included stronger global functional connectivity and changes in the usual separation of networks.

Functional connectivity describes statistical relationships between signals recorded from different regions. It is not a photograph of new wires growing between neurons. Stronger connectivity also does not mean that every region becomes more active or that more of an otherwise unused brain suddenly switches on. The finding concerns the organization of measured activity during a particular state.

One way to approach the result is to think about the usual balance between specialized processing and communication across systems. Under DMT, some familiar boundaries become less distinct. That may help researchers investigate why perception, emotion and the sense of self change together. It does not yet explain every image or every apparent conversation someone reports.

An earlier study of electrical activity found changes in rhythmic patterns, including reduced alpha activity, increased signal diversity and the emergence of slower oscillations during peak effects. The researchers discussed possible similarities with dreaming. They also treated the interpretation as a hypothesis. Finding some shared features does not mean that a DMT experience is simply an ordinary dream with another name.

The comparison is nevertheless useful. During a dream, an internally generated scene can seem convincing while it lasts. We do not normally stop every few seconds to ask who built the room or why an unlikely event is happening. DMT raises related questions about how experience becomes convincing, although the conditions and reported intensity can differ substantially.

Metaphors can help explain these findings, but they can also distort them. Calling the brain more democratic or anarchic may suggest a change in organization. Saying that it burns out or decides it is dying sounds like a measured biological event. That is not what a connectivity analysis demonstrates. A vivid metaphor should not take the place of the result.

The molecule inside the body

Another source of fascination is evidence that DMT can occur naturally in mammals, including humans. Natural occurrence does not tell us its function, and it does not mean that adding more is safe. A substance can have different effects depending on where it is present, how much is present and the circumstances in which it acts.

A 2019 study investigated enzymes involved in DMT synthesis in rat and human tissues. It also measured DMT directly in the brains of rats. After experimentally induced cardiac arrest, concentrations increased in the rat brain regions studied. The researchers found evidence that did not depend on an intact pineal gland.

Those are animal findings, alongside human tissue observations about enzyme expression. They do not establish that a dying human brain releases enough DMT to cause visions. Nor do they prove that DMT produces spiritual experiences at birth or acts as the body's emergency medicine. Moving from a measured substance to an explanation of human consciousness requires additional evidence.

The uncertainty is not a failure to notice something obvious. There are several separate questions. Can a tissue produce the substance? What concentration is present during normal activity? Does that concentration affect nearby cells? What changes under stress? Each question requires a suitable measurement, and an answer to one does not automatically answer the others.

Experiences near death

Some reports under DMT resemble descriptions given by people who felt close to death. Both can involve altered time, unusual light, separation from the body or a sense of profound meaning. Comparing these descriptions may help researchers ask more precise questions about experience. It does not show that the causes are identical.

A small study published in 2018 compared questionnaire responses after DMT with features associated with experiences near death. Thirteen healthy volunteers took part in the laboratory study. The researchers found substantial overlap, but the study had limitations, including a fixed order in which placebo came first. The participants were not actually dying.

That distinction cannot be removed by an intense feeling of danger or transcendence. A questionnaire measures reported features. It does not measure a person's proximity to another world or establish the chemical cause of an event during cardiac arrest. Similar descriptions can be a starting point for research without being its final explanation.

For someone who has had such an experience, the personal significance may remain even when the mechanism is uncertain. Science does not need to dismiss that significance to investigate it. It does need to distinguish an account of meaning from a claim about how the brain produced the experience.

Hope and clinical evidence

The possibility of medical benefit has drawn new attention to DMT and related research. A powerful experience can leave someone feeling different afterwards. The clinical question is more demanding: does a treatment improve symptoms, for whom, for how long, and with what harms? An emotionally compelling account cannot answer those questions alone.

A small randomized trial published in February 2026 compared DMT with placebo in thirty four adults with depression, with psychological support. At two weeks, symptoms improved more in the DMT group. Later follow up involved an open phase, so it did not provide the same continuing comparison with placebo.

The trial did not assess whether participants correctly guessed their assignment or measure their expectations. Its sample was limited, and support was part of the intervention. The findings therefore cannot establish what would happen without that support or rule out uncommon harms. The study was sponsored by Small Pharma, and several authors disclosed commercial relationships. These details belong beside the promising result.

More broadly, research on depression should not be treated as proof of effectiveness for every condition mentioned in stories about psychedelics. Trauma, anxiety and depression can overlap in someone's life, but they are not interchangeable research outcomes. A result in carefully selected participants also does not establish that a commercial retreat offers the same treatment conditions.

There are risks to consider alongside possible benefits. Psychedelic experiences can involve severe fear, confusion and impaired judgment. Physical effects and persistent perceptual symptoms are also concerns. Screening, monitoring and follow up in a study are part of the context in which its results were obtained. They are not incidental details that can be removed while keeping every conclusion unchanged.

An unfinished conversation

DMT brings several kinds of question into the same room. Chemists ask what a molecule does. Neuroscientists ask how patterns of brain activity change. Clinicians ask whether an intervention helps patients. People who have had an unusual experience ask what it means for their lives. The questions can inform one another, but they require different kinds of evidence.

The documentary returns to the Amazon with a modern researcher studying ayahuasca. The contrast with Spruce is striking, but it should not become a story in which modern equipment has solved everything. Identifying ingredients and measuring activity have made some questions more precise. The relationship between chemistry, culture and experience remains complicated.

After a laboratory session, the participant is again in the room where the experience began. The researcher can record what was said, compare measurements and arrange the next assessment. There may be an account of terror, relief, a strange figure or a deeply felt connection. Understanding it requires listening carefully, then asking which part can be tested and what evidence the next study would need.
