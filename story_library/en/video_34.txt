Hidden in a tablet

A death behind a closed door

The documentary opens in a home in England. Jade wakes during the night and notices that her partner, Owen, is not beside her. She finds him unconscious in the bathroom. She calls for help and tries to revive him, but the emergency team cannot save his life.

This is the account presented by the documentary. It says Owen had previously struggled with drug dependence and had secretly ordered drugs through the post. It also reports that toxicology identified a synthetic opioid alongside other substances. The underlying medical record has not been established for this adaptation, so the account should not be read as an independently verified reconstruction of every detail.

The wider danger it introduces is documented. Nitazenes, a family of synthetic opioids, have appeared in illicit drugs and counterfeit medicines. A person may believe they are taking one substance while receiving another. The consequences can become apparent only when someone loses consciousness or stops breathing normally.

From a distance, drug markets can seem like an argument about borders, laboratories and police seizures. Inside a home, the problem is immediate. A familiar person is in danger, and those around them may not know what they have taken. That gap between appearance and contents connects a private emergency to changes far beyond the room.

A farmer in the documentary

The film then introduces Ahmed, a fictional farmer used to illustrate conditions in Afghanistan. He is not a documented interview subject whose personal history has been independently confirmed. His story represents a practical dilemma: how does a household support itself when its most profitable crop is prohibited?

In the illustration, Ahmed has invested in land, a well and equipment for irrigation. The field did not become productive without effort or expense. Water has to reach the crops, equipment has to work, and the family needs income between one harvest and the next. These ordinary farming pressures help explain why replacing a crop can be difficult.

Ahmed grows opium poppies as well as food for his household. Opium is part of the supply chain for heroin. For a family dependent on the income, a prohibition changes much more than the appearance of a field. It can affect debts, food purchases and the ability to maintain the farm.

The real historical change came after the Taliban returned to power in August 2021. In April 2022, they announced a ban on poppy cultivation and other drug activity. United Nations monitoring subsequently recorded a steep reduction in Afghan cultivation and opium production. Farmers who had relied on the crop faced a serious loss of income.

That decline did not prove that Afghan opium had disappeared permanently. On 29 October 2026, the United Nations reported a substantial rebound in production, although levels remained below those before the ban. The change is a reminder that a market forecast can become outdated. Poverty, enforcement and available alternatives continue to affect what farmers grow.

Why a harvest is not a market

A sudden reduction in production naturally raises questions about supply elsewhere. Afghanistan had long been an important source of the heroin sold in Europe. If less opium was harvested, would heroin become scarcer? And if it did, what would sellers offer instead?

The answer was not immediate. Stored opium could continue to enter the supply chain after fields stopped producing. Traders and suppliers in different places might hold reserves. Other regions might increase production. A change at the beginning of a supply chain does not arrive at every destination at the same time.

The documentary interviews researcher Daniel Brombacher about these uncertainties. He describes efforts to understand whether synthetic opioids already have a market, who buys them and how they are sold. The useful question is not simply whether a feared crisis will happen. It is what evidence can reveal about changes already taking place.

European drug monitoring has treated a shift towards synthetic opioids as a risk requiring preparation, not as an inevitable outcome across the continent. A shortage can create opportunities for substitutes, but markets differ. Buyers, sellers, treatment services and existing drug patterns all affect what follows.

The distinction matters for public communication. Saying that nothing will change may encourage complacency. Saying that a catastrophe is certain can make uncertainty disappear from the story. Neither helps people interpret the evidence. Authorities need to watch changes in availability, composition and harm without pretending to know the future.

An old family of substances

Nitazenes did not begin as a recent invention for online markets. They were developed during pharmaceutical research in the 1950s. They were never approved as medicines. Decades later, members of this family appeared in illicit drug supplies, where quality and contents cannot be assumed from a label.

The name covers different substances, not one uniform drug. Some can match or exceed the potency of fentanyl. A single comparison cannot describe every nitazene, every mixture or every person exposed to it. Dramatic claims about one quantity being able to kill a city obscure these differences rather than explain them.

The central health danger is easier to state. Like other opioids, nitazenes can suppress breathing. Severe poisoning can be fatal. A person who does not know an opioid is present may also have no tolerance to its effects. Uncertainty about the contents makes it harder to recognise the risk before an emergency occurs.

This is why the appearance of a tablet is a poor guide to safety. A counterfeit can resemble a familiar prescription medicine without containing the expected ingredient or a reliable amount of it. The familiarity of the shape or name does not establish how it was manufactured.

European reports have described nitazenes in products sold as heroin and in counterfeit medicines resembling opioid painkillers or sedatives. These are not all the same audience. Some people knowingly seek an opioid. Others believe they have obtained a different drug. Warnings that address only the first group can miss people in the second.

The screen and the parcel

The documentary examines the role of online selling. Its team reports contacting a seller but stopping before completing a purchase. That illustrates an attempted transaction, not laboratory confirmation of a product that arrived. A seller's claim is not proof of a parcel's contents.

There is no need to repeat the purchasing process to understand the concern. Online contact can connect buyers and sellers who never meet. The eventual physical delivery may be only one part of a relationship conducted through messages. That can make the people involved less visible to services accustomed to working in established local drug scenes.

Synthetic production also differs from agriculture. It does not depend on a poppy harvest in the same way as heroin production does. That difference can create commercial incentives for illicit suppliers. It does not mean that production is effortless, that every transaction takes place online or that all sellers will inevitably abandon other drugs.

The documentary contrasts these markets with longer supply chains involving farmers, processors, importers and local dealers. The comparison helps explain why one policing approach may not fit every substance. But real markets can combine different routes and intermediaries. A simple diagram should not be mistaken for a complete map.

For prevention, the question becomes how to reach someone who does not think of themselves as an opioid user. A warning delivered at a local service may never reach a person ordering what they believe is a sedative. That is a challenge for communication, not proof that communication is useless.

What the figures can miss

European monitoring has reported serious harm from nitazenes, with particular concern in the Baltic countries. Yet a list of deaths from different countries can mislead if the years, testing methods and definitions do not match. A small recorded number is not automatically a complete count.

Laboratories must know what they are looking for and use methods capable of detecting it. New substances can be missed by procedures designed for older drugs. This creates a problem for both an individual investigation and the broader picture. If a substance is not identified, the warning signal may be weaker than the actual harm.

A negative test for one substance is not a guarantee about everything else. Fentanyl test strips, for example, do not establish whether nitazenes are absent. Even when a sample is analysed, the result has limits. It should not be turned into a promise that using an illicit product is safe.

Seizure figures require care too. They tell us what authorities found, which depends partly on enforcement priorities and detection. They do not directly reveal the total amount consumed or every route used. An increase can reflect a changing market, improved detection or both.

Better information depends on connections between laboratories, emergency services, treatment providers and warning systems. An unusual poisoning seen in one place may matter elsewhere. Sharing a useful alert quickly can be more valuable than waiting until the problem is large enough to dominate the news.

Treatment still matters

One claim in the original account needs a clear correction. Naloxone can reverse an opioid overdose involving nitazenes. It should not be described as an antidote that works for heroin and fentanyl but fails against this family of substances. Public health guidance includes nitazenes among the opioids for which naloxone is used.

Some emergencies may require repeated doses. That does not make the medicine pointless. It means that an apparent response must not be confused with the end of the emergency. Breathing problems can return, and medical help remains essential. The appropriate response is urgent assistance, not an attempt to identify the exact molecule before acting.

If someone is unresponsive or is not breathing normally, call emergency services immediately. Use available naloxone according to its instructions and follow the dispatcher's guidance. Stay with the person until help arrives. These basic actions belong alongside the discussion of markets because a warning is more useful when people know that help can make a difference.

Access to naloxone and treatment varies between places. Rules about supplying a medicine, local services and practical availability are separate issues. A country may permit an intervention without making it easy for everyone who needs it to obtain it. That gap deserves attention without relying on an outdated list of national rules.

People living with dependence also need more than an emergency response. Treatment, reliable contact with health services and support without humiliation can create opportunities to reduce harm. None of these measures guarantees an outcome. Together, they offer more than waiting for the next arrest or poisoning report.

Before the next warning

The story began with a partner finding someone unconscious at home. The documentary's account gives that danger a personal setting. The broader evidence shows why the concern cannot be confined to one person, one country or one kind of drug buyer.

Afghan farming, synthetic substances and online markets are connected, but they do not form a single inevitable sequence. A harvest can fall and later recover. A seller can change products. A laboratory can identify a substance that earlier tests missed. Preparation depends on noticing those changes and responding to what they show.

The practical work is often less dramatic than the headlines: updating testing, sharing alerts, making treatment accessible and helping people recognise an overdose. It also means speaking clearly about uncertainty. A familiar tablet may not contain a familiar medicine, and an apparently small market can still cause devastating harm.

The aim is not to make every reader memorise a catalogue of chemicals. It is to make an unfamiliar risk understandable before another household encounters it without warning. Behind the statistics are people who can still be reached, treated and helped.
