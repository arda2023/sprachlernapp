"Harry Potter, Game of Thrones, Batman, Superman, Bugs Bunny, and CNN all just got a new owner. And the company that bought them was worth about a tenth of the price it paid. On October 6th, 2026, Paramount closed its deal to buy Warner Brothers Discovery for about 110 billion dollars. A few months earlier, the stock market valued the whole of Paramount at around 12 billion. That's a bit like the corner shop buying the supermarket across the street along with its parking lot, its delivery trucks, and the bank that lent it money. So, how does a company buy something nine times bigger than itself? The answer says a lot about how modern business really works, who has the money, who borrows it, and who ends up in control. But to get there, we have to start with a plane crash. A fake one. In 2006, a movie called Flyboys came out. It's a First World War story about young American pilots and it cost about 60 million dollars to make. Worldwide, it brought back about 18 million. One of its stars was a 23-year-old named David Ellison, who wasn't just acting in the film, he was also one of the people who paid for it. Critics were not kind and the acting career never really took off. Most people in that position would get a hug and some advice about a backup plan. David Ellison's backup plan was a little stronger than most because his father is Larry Ellison, the co-founder of the software giant Oracle and one of the richest people on the planet. That same year, David started his own production company and called it Skydance. And to be fair to him, this part worked. Skydance became a partner on big studio films including the Mission Impossible series and later Top Gun Maverick, which made more than a billion dollars at the box office. The young man whose career as a movie pilot crashed went on to help make one of the most successful pilot movies ever made. Then, in 2025, he went shopping. The Ellison family and an investment firm called RedBird Capital put in about eight billion dollars to take control of Paramount. That gave David the Paramount film studio, the CBS television network, Nickelodeon, MTV, Comedy Central, and the streaming service Paramount Plus. He became the boss of one of Hollywood's oldest studios, and almost immediately he started looking at a much bigger one, because Warner Brothers was about to go up for sale. Warner Brothers is no stranger to being sold. It was founded in 1923 by four brothers, Harry, Albert, Sam, and Jack Warner. And over the next 100 years, it gave the world talking pictures, Bugs Bunny, Casablanca, and eventually Harry Potter. But the brothers sold out in the 1960s, and since then the studio has passed through the hands of half a dozen owners. One of them started out running parking lots and funeral homes. Another was the magazine giant Time, which brought in HBO. Then came the internet company AOL, and what was then the biggest merger in history. In 1989, when Warner merged with Time, another company even tried to break up the deal with a hostile bid of its own. Its name was Paramount, and it lost. The last owner before all this was the phone company AT&T, which bought the business in 2018 for about $85 billion, and renamed it Warner Media. But AT&T was already carrying an enormous amount of debt, and just 4 years later it gave up on owning a movie studio. In 2022, it merged Warner Media with Discovery, the company behind HGTV, Food Network, and the Discovery Channel. The new company, Warner Brothers Discovery, owned one of the biggest collections of stories in the world. It also started life with more than $43 billion of debt. That debt became a problem, because the most reliable part of the business was shrinking. For decades, cable channels were a money machine. Every month, millions of households paid for a cable bundle, whether they watched TNT and the Food Network or not. As people canceled cable and moved to streaming, that money started to disappear. By early 2025, Warner Brothers Discovery stock had lost more than 60% of its value. So, in June of that year, the company announced it would split itself in two, the studios and HBO on one side and the old cable channels on the other. That plan put a sign on the door that read, more or less, open for offers. And 36 years after it lost the fight for Time Warner, Paramount was the first one through it. In September 2025, Paramount offered about $19 a share, partly in cash and partly in its own stock. Warner's board said no. Paramount came back with $22 and then with $23.50. Each time the answer was no. Warner's board believed the company was worth more. And it knew another buyer was interested. In December, that other buyer signed a deal. Netflix agreed to buy the Warner Brothers film and TV studios, HBO and HBO Max in a deal worth about $83 billion. But Netflix only wanted the good parts. CNN, TNT, Discovery, and the rest of the cable channels would be split off into a separate company. Warner shareholders would keep that company along with a large share of the debt. Netflix wanted Harry Potter, not HGTV. That detail turned out Paramount's argument to shareholders was simple. Netflix is paying you for the best half and leaving you holding the half that's shrinking along with the bills. We'll pay you cash for everything. Three days after the Netflix deal, Paramount stopped asking Warner's board and went straight to the shareholders. It offered to buy their shares directly for $30 each, all in cash. On Wall Street, this is called a hostile tender offer. In normal language, it's knocking on the front door, being told no, and climbing in through the window with a bag of money. The shareholders didn't rush to the window. By late January, only a small fraction of Warner's shares had been offered to Paramount, far from the majority it needed. A cash offer is only as good as the cash behind it, and many investors weren't sure Paramount could actually pay. So, Paramount kept adding to the bag. Larry Ellison signed an irrevocable personal guarantee for more than $40 billion of the money. Paramount promised that if Warner had to break its contract with Netflix, Paramount would pay the $2.8 billion dollar fee itself. It even offered shareholders a kind of late fee. If the deal hadn't closed by the end of 2026, Paramount would pay them about 650 million dollars for every extra 3 months of waiting. Then in February 2026, Paramount raised its price to $31 a share. Warner's board finally called it the better offer and Netflix declined to go higher. The next day, Paramount paid Netflix its 2.8 billion dollars and Netflix's own stock jumped about 14%. Its investors were celebrating the deal it didn't get. So, Paramount had won, which brings us back to the problem from the beginning. A company worth around 12 billion dollars had just agreed to a 110 billion dollar purchase. Where does that money actually come from? About 80 billion of it is the price for Warner's shares. The rest is Warner's existing debt, which Paramount takes over along with the company. When you buy a business, you buy its bills, too. The second thing to know is that a deal like this works much like buying a house. Nobody pays the full price from their savings. You bring a down payment and a bank lends you the rest. Paramount just did it with a lot more zeros. The down payment came from new shares. Paramount raised about 4.7 billion dollars by selling new stock to a small group of investors. The Ellison family bought about 17 billion dollars of it. Around 24 billion came from the sovereign wealth funds of Saudi Arabia, Qatar, and the United Arab Emirates. RedBird and a few other investors added the rest. The mortgage came from the banks. Bank of America, Citigroup, and the investment firm Apollo committed about 54 billion dollars in loans. Most of it started as a huge short-term bridge loan, which Paramount planned to replace with longer-term debt once the deal was done. And here is the part that lets a smaller company buy a bigger one. Paramount isn't paying the mortgage out of its own pocket. It's like buying a house, renting it out, and paying the mortgage with the rent the house brings in. In this deal, Warner Brothers is that house. The loans are paid back with the money Warner itself earns. The box office, the HBO subscriptions, the advertising on CNN and TNT. Warner is the thing being bought, and it is also the thing paying for it. There's one more twist, and it's about control. The three gold funds put in more money than the Ellisons did, but their shares come with no votes. According to Paramount's own filing with American regulators, the new company will be almost half foreign-owned. Yet every single voting share sits with the Ellison family and RedBird. The investors who brought the most cash don't get to run the company. The family that brought less does. Now, why would banks and governments put that much money behind this plan? Because Warner Brothers is very good at the exact thing Paramount wants to be good at. In 2025, Warner Brothers films took about 21% of the North American box office. Paramount's took 6%. That year, Warner's films earned 30 Oscar nominations. Paramount's earned zero. In other words, Paramount didn't win this by being the best student in the class. It failed the exam, and then it bought the student with the top mark. But top students don't come for free, and this one came with student loans. Once the deal closed, the new company was carrying roughly 80 billion dollars of debt. That's almost double the 43 billion dollars that pushed Warner into this situation in the first place. That's more than four times what the business earns in a year before interest and taxes. The plan is to bring it down to about three times by the end of 2029. To get there, the new owner wants to cut around six billion dollars of costs a year. In Hollywood, it usually means fewer jobs and fewer projects that don't look like guaranteed hits. That worried a lot of people. The deal needed approval in the United States and 67 other countries. California, 11 other states, and the Writers Guild of America went to court to try to stop it. And for a few weeks in July, a judge put the deal on hold. To settle, Paramount agreed to release at least 30 films a year and to keep new movies in cinemas for 45 days before they go to streaming. It also agreed to spend 1.5 billion dollars on production in the United States over 5 years. For every promised film it fails to release, it pays a 30 million dollar penalty. It also agreed to set up an independent board to protect the news at CBS and CNN. And the man who ran Warner Bros. Discovery, David Zaslav, is expected to make more than 550 million dollars from the sale. Selling the company might be the most profitable thing he did there. On October 6th, the deal finally closed. Paramount and Warner Bros. Discovery became one company with a new name, Skydance. That's the name of the little production company David Ellison started the same year his acting career crashed. So, that's how a smaller company buys a giant. It borrows most of the money, brings in investors who are happy to stay quiet, and leans on a father rich enough to guarantee the rest. Then, it sends the bill to the company it just bought. And if you pay for both Paramount Plus and HBO Max, a small part of every monthly payment now goes to the same owner and to that same pile of debt. And as for the man at the top of all this, 20 years ago, David Ellison was an actor in a 60 million dollar flop about pilots. Today, his company owns Batman, Harry Potter, and CNN.
