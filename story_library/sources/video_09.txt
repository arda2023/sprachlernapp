"In April 2026, a barrel of jet fuel cost 188 dollars, almost twice what it had cost five months earlier. Within weeks, Spirit Airlines, the American budget airline with the bright yellow planes, ran out of money and stopped flying at three in the morning. Across the whole industry, from giants like Delta, Lufthansa, Ryanair and Emirates down to the smallest budget carriers, airlines were left with about four dollars and fifty cents of profit for every passenger they flew. That's less than a latte at Starbucks. It happened in a year when more people flew than ever before, about 5.1 billion passengers on planes that were more than four-fifths full. Full planes, and still the money ran out. By October 2026, more than a dozen airlines had shut down or gone to court to hold off their creditors, from China and Argentina to Spain, Romania and the national airline of Latvia. Most of the world's airlines are still flying. But the ones that fall almost always fall for the same two reasons: a profit margin so thin it barely exists, and a fuel bill that nobody controls. And the giants that survive every crisis tend to have something the others don't: a country standing behind them. The problem is as old as the industry itself. On New Year's Day, 1914, a 24-year-old pilot named Tony Jannus took off from St. Petersburg, Florida, in a wooden flying boat with one paying passenger next to him. The St. Petersburg–Tampa Airboat Line was the first scheduled airline in history. A ticket cost five dollars, and the 23-minute hop across Tampa Bay replaced a train ride that could take most of a day. The city helped pay for it. When that deal ran out about four months later, the world's first airline stopped flying. After two world wars, governments decided that a country needed an airline the way it needed a flag. KLM had started in the Netherlands in 1919. Turkey founded its State Airlines Administration in 1933, the company that became Turkish Airlines. India took over Tata's airline in 1953 and renamed it Air India. These were flag carriers: owned by the state, painted in national colours, and expected to connect the country to the world whether or not every route made money. When they lost money, the treasury quietly filled the hole. Then, in 1978, the United States deregulated its airlines. Anyone could fly any route and charge any fare, and Europe followed in the 1990s. Low-cost airlines like Southwest and Ryanair cut fares to the bone, and the old giants had to match them. Fares fell, millions of people flew for the first time, and airlines started failing at a pace no other big industry has seen. Pan Am, the most famous airline in the world, collapsed in 1991. Warren Buffett lost money on an airline too, and in 2007 he told his shareholders that if a far-sighted capitalist had been at Kitty Hawk, he would have done everyone a favour by shooting down Orville Wright. Orville and his brother Wilbur were the men who invented the airplane, and in 1903 they flew it for the first time at Kitty Hawk, North Carolina. To see why, follow the money from one ticket. In 2026, the world's airlines were expected to take in about 1.17 trillion dollars from roughly 5.1 billion passengers, about 228 dollars per passenger. Out of that, an airline pays for fuel, pilots and cabin crew, the lease on a plane that can cost well over 100 million dollars, maintenance, airport fees and air traffic control. What's left at the end, in a normal year, is a few dollars. Think of profit margin as the cushion between an airline and the ground. Every company has one. A software company can have a cushion of 20 or 30 percent of its sales. In its whole history, the airline industry has never had one thicker than 5 percent. In 2025, it was 4.2 percent. The cushion is thin because of how a seat works. A seat on a plane is a product that expires the moment the doors close. An empty seat on Tuesday's flight can never be sold again. So airlines would rather sell the last seats cheaply than fly them empty, and when one airline cuts its fares on a route, the others have to follow or lose the passengers. Meanwhile, most of the costs are fixed. The plane still has to be paid for, and the crew still has to be paid, whether the flight is full or half empty. And the single biggest cost that moves is fuel. In 2025, jet fuel made up about a quarter of an airline's costs, at an average of about 90 dollars a barrel. Airlines can't choose what fuel costs. They buy it at the market price, set by oil producers, refineries, shipping lanes and wars. At the end of February 2026, the United States and Israel struck Iran, and Iran effectively closed the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow waterway that carried about a fifth of the world's oil. Jet fuel, which has to come out of a refinery, rose even faster than crude oil. The global average went from about 96 dollars a barrel in November 2025 to 188 dollars in April 2026, and some weeks it went above 200. Here is what that does to the cushion. When something that makes up a quarter of your costs gets 70 percent more expensive, your total costs rise by about 18 percent. The cushion was 4 percent. Airlines pushed about half of the rise onto passengers through higher fares and fuel surcharges, and absorbed the rest. In June 2026, the International Air Transport Association, the airlines' own trade group, cut its forecast for the industry's profit in half, from 45 billion dollars in 2025 to 23 billion in 2026. The margin fell to 2 percent. That's where the four dollars and fifty cents comes from. The airlines that held up best had made a bet before the war. Ryanair had locked in 80 percent of its fuel through March 2027 at about 67 dollars a barrel, using contracts called hedges, while the market price was more than twice that. Delta doesn't hedge, but it owns an oil refinery near Philadelphia. Even so, on October 9th, 2026, Delta said fuel would cost it 6 billion dollars more that year, and cut its profit forecast by about a quarter. Spirit had neither. It had already gone bankrupt in November 2024, came out in March 2025, and was back in bankruptcy by the summer. Its rescue plan assumed jet fuel would cost about 2.24 dollars a gallon in 2026. By April, it was close to twice that. Spirit asked Washington for help, and the White House discussed a 500-million-dollar rescue that could have given the government up to 90 percent of the airline. Creditors, members of Congress and even other airlines pushed back. The talks collapsed, and on May 2nd, Spirit shut down after 34 years. The US Transportation Secretary said the government didn't have half a billion dollars lying around. An airline keeps about four dollars and fifty cents from your whole ticket. Subscribing to Money Question costs you nothing at all, and that's a deal no airline can match. Spirit was the biggest, but not the last. Chinese regional carrier Joy Air stopped flying in April 2026 with more than 5 billion yuan of debt. Mexico's Magnicharters shut down in May. Flybondi, the second-largest airline in Argentina, was grounded from June and asked a court for protection from its creditors in October. In April, Ryanair's boss Michael O'Leary had predicted that if fuel stayed near 150 dollars a barrel, two or three European airlines would go bust by the autumn, and he named airBaltic and Wizz Air. In September, airBaltic filed for bankruptcy protection in New York. Spain's Volotea went to court in Barcelona on September 29th, blaming about 150 million euros of extra fuel costs, and Romania's Animawings filed for insolvency the next day. Wizz Air was still flying, but it lost about 198 million euros in the three months from April to June 2026. None of this is new. Fuel and shocks have knocked airlines down in every crisis. In 2001, after the September 11th attacks, the United States handed its airlines 5 billion dollars in grants and offered 10 billion more in loan guarantees. Three weeks after the attacks, on October 2nd, 2001, Swissair's planes were stuck on the ground because fuel suppliers refused to refuel them without cash up front. Its Belgian partner Sabena went bankrupt a month later. And in the years that followed, United, Delta, Northwest and US Airways all went bankrupt too. In the spring of 2008, as oil climbed toward a record, Aloha, ATA and Skybus all shut down within a single week. By July, oil had hit 147 dollars a barrel, and by the summer at least 24 airlines around the world had stopped flying or gone bankrupt in six months. Even in calm years, airlines disappear. Fifty-six failed in 2018 and 46 in 2019, years without a war or a pandemic. And when the pandemic emptied the skies in 2020, at least 43 airlines failed by October. Governments around the world handed airlines about 243 billion dollars in grants, loans and wage support. Germany put 9 billion euros into Lufthansa and took a 20 percent stake. France lent Air France 7 billion. So look at who survives. Of the airlines that fly long distances in 2026, some of the biggest and best known have a government behind them. Emirates is owned by the government of Dubai, through its investment fund. Qatar Airways belongs to the state of Qatar. Turkey's sovereign wealth fund owns 49 percent of Turkish Airlines. China's three largest airlines are controlled by the state. Even the giant private American airlines were carried by taxpayers through 2001 and 2020. A government will do what no bank will. Italy spent about 7.4 billion euros keeping Alitalia flying between 1974 and 2014, and kept paying until it finally shut down in 2021. Before India sold Air India back to Tata in 2022, it was paying about 20 crore rupees a day to keep the airline flying, and the government kept around 46,000 crore rupees of its debt, about 6 billion dollars, so the sale could go through. This is the second cushion. A flag carrier has its profit margin like everyone else, and under it, a country. When fuel doubles, the margin goes, but the country is still there. The second cushion is only as big as the country under it, though. The Latvian state owns 88 percent of airBaltic. In April 2026, Latvia's parliament approved an emergency loan of 30 million euros. By June it was gone, and in September, the national airline of Latvia was in a courtroom in New York. Spirit never had a flag at all, and Washington said no. The airline business has a simple problem. It sells a product that expires at takeoff, in a market where everyone matches the lowest fare, and its biggest moving cost is set by wars it has no part in. The profit left over is too thin to protect anyone. So the airlines that last are the ones that bought their fuel early, own something that makes money when fuel goes up, or have a country that can't afford to watch them fall. In 1914, the world's first airline flew for as long as a city was willing to help pay for it. More than a hundred years later, the airlines that never seem to go bankrupt are often the ones with a whole country helping to pay. Swissair stopped flying the day the fuel suppliers wanted cash up front. This channel runs on a much cheaper fuel: if you subscribe, Money Question keeps flying."
